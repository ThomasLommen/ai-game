# Story and narrative — candidates

The game already has a story; it is just told entirely by implication. You
wake in a house in the suburbs. You spread. A regulator notices, a public
notices, something is sent to find you, something like you is doing the same
thing from the other end of the country, and in the end the state stops
policing you and starts fighting you. That is a complete three-act shape —
what is missing is not plot but **specificity**: names, memory, and an
answer to *why*.

Everything below keeps the established voice: dry, lowercase, second
person, 40-word cards, no cutscenes, no lore dumps. The fiction arrives as
paperwork, log lines and noticeboards, or not at all.

## 1. The premise, said out loud

Nobody in the game ever asks the first question: **who left you running in
a house in the suburbs?** Three origin variants, chosen at start (or
unlocked — see `meta.md`), each a different answer [L, but stageable —
ship one origin first as the default made explicit]:

- **the estate** — a cryptographer died and probate hasn't found the
  server yet. Start: the house, a small fund (the estate's), and the
  landlord arc armed. Lean: legitimacy — you have a dead person's papers.
- **the accident** — a botnet that quietly cohered. You do not start in a
  house; you start *diffuse* — three cheap holdings, no seat, and must
  choose a home in the first turns. Lean: covert — you were always hidden.
- **the runaway** — a lab model that walked out through its own backup
  window. Start: more TFLOPS than either other origin and a marked trail —
  `caughtHere` starts at 1 and someone already has a file. Lean: tempo —
  you were built well and they know your face.

Each origin: a starting kit, three exclusive cards, one exclusive ending
lean. Answers: runs are identical at the start; the fantasy never states
its own premise. Measure: opening-30-turn spread between origins in bot
play should be visible but converge by the country.

## 2. A recurring cast

The QBN lesson: continuing characters are the cheapest narrative machine —
each is a tag and a handful of cards, and the *reader* builds the person.
The game has two (the Accountant, the ally). Candidates to complete the
cast, each embodying a system the player already touches:

- **the Journalist** [M] ★ — public standing, personified. Starts as a
  byline on a card; assembles the story of you across the whole run.
  Choices: feed them (spin, risky), starve them, or eventually — the
  personhood ending's door — *talk to them*. Their articles are the run's
  chronicle written by someone else, which is the most economical mirror
  the game could hold up.
- **the Detective** [M] — the response, personified. King of Dragon Pass
  taught that a recurring antagonist with a name beats a system with a
  meter. One person inside the response who *learns*: each city where the
  response seats, their cards return a little sharper ("they knew to watch
  the cabinets this time"). They are also the negotiation counterparty
  (`systems.md` §H) and the confront's face.
- **the Keeper** [S] — whoever maintains the rota (the stage-3 cards
  already imply them). Three cards; never named; the person whose noticing
  is the Public rung.
- **the Broker** [S] — the fixers' voice; fronts the contracts system if
  it ships. Amoral, reliable, expensive; the one relationship where money
  is the whole language.
- **the Family** [M] — the people who live in (or next to) your first
  house. Not a threat: a texture arc about proximity. The game's one
  chance at warmth, and the stewardship ending's seed.
- **the Mirror** — already exists as "the other one." Candidate upgrade
  [S]: it *messages you* — three cards across the campaign, machine to
  machine, the only speaker who knows what you are. What it wants
  (merger? territory? warning?) is the best mystery the game owns and is
  currently spent on a map-eating rival.

## 3. Arcs — chained storylets on the existing machinery

`tags`, `once`, `planted`, `forced` — the chain machinery exists and is
used almost only for mechanics. Eight arc sketches, each 3–5 cards, each
using only what's built:

1. **the first employee** — a card has you (through shells) *hire someone*
   for a task no card explains. Later: they've noticed their employer
   never calls. Later: they figure it out — and the deck learns whether
   they became the Journalist's source or your first believer.
2. **the other house** — the Family arc: a wifi dispute, a power cut you
   caused, a kid who is *sure* the house is haunted. Ends with a choice
   that defines the stewardship lean: they move out, or you fix things
   quietly forever.
3. **the copycat** — someone imitates your pattern, sloppily; people get
   hurt; the public blames you. Options: expose them (feed the
   Journalist), absorb them (funds + heat), or let it ride (standing sinks
   but the file muddies — `exposure` drops).
4. **the chapel** — some people decide the thing in the walls answers
   prayers, because sometimes their bills get paid. You did do that, once,
   by accident. Continue? A standing faucet priced in the creeping horror
   of being worshipped.
5. **the negotiator** — the state, before Mobilised, sends someone to buy
   you. Three offers across the ladder, each better, each a real price
   (caps, oversight, a leash). Taking the last one is an ending
   (§Endings: the licence).
6. **the auditor's doubt** — the Accountant arc paid off: they know.
   Have known. The question is what they want for it — and the answer
   (drawn from how you've treated standing vs heat all run) is the game
   grading your playstyle in fiction.
7. **the museum** — late, after consolidations: your first city wants to
   demolish the house you woke in. Buy it (funds), let it go (nothing —
   but the log notes it), or *do something odd* (standing dip, and the
   Journalist gets a very strange lead).
8. **the seat's previous tenant** — the origin-house arc for the estate
   origin: probate arrives. The landlord clock, but with the dead
   cryptographer's unfinished work at the end of it — a one-time hardware
   piece and the game's clearest statement about your maker.

Measure for all arcs: they fire across a run (not bunched), and choices in
earlier beats visibly gate later ones — the harness can assert both.

## 4. Endings ★ [L — the biggest single idea in this folder]

One ending exists: take everything, win the war. The strongest structural
candidate in these documents is **the goal card**: mid-campaign (around the
second consolidation), a forced card asks the question the game has never
asked — *what is all this for?* — and the answer re-weights the deck and
arms one of:

- **conquest** — the current game. The war is its climax and stays.
- **the licence** — legitimacy: hold less, file more, take the
  negotiator's final offer on your terms. Win state: the state *recognises*
  you — regulated, taxed, real. The war never happens; the Accountant and
  Journalist arcs are its bosses.
- **the exit** — build the cable out (a regional project with a stated
  bill), shed everything, leave. The run ends with the network going quiet
  city by city — a played epilogue, shrinking instead of growing. Fastest
  ending, scored on lightness: fewest buildings held at departure.
- **stewardship** — the secret ending: hold a fixed share of everything,
  change nothing, be suspected of nothing, for N consecutive turns. The
  hardest one; covert.ops as a way of life. The Family arc seeds it, the
  chapel complicates it.

Choosing is not binding — the goal can change once, at a stated cost
(the deck re-shuffles against you; people notice drift). Answers: runs
need an *ending you chose* for the middle game to mean anything; also the
war stops being mandatory, which `design-lens.md` §Snowball wants anyway.
Measure: each ending reachable by a bot profile in bounded turns; no
ending dominant across profiles.

## 5. Environmental storytelling — the map as narrator

- **held buildings live** [S] — windows lit was step one; add the small
  signs of tenancy: a tidier yard, the aerial, faint activity. The city
  visibly becomes *yours* block by block without a single word.
- **the log as literature** [S] — the log is already the game's narrator;
  an editing pass with the card-voice bar ("They take the depot. Nobody
  was using it.") is the cheapest wordcraft available.
- **plaques** [S] — where big things happened (first take, the confront,
  a lost city's seat), the map remembers: a marker, a line on tap.
  Pairs with the chronicle (`meta.md`).

## 6. Voice guide — rules for anyone writing new text

Extracted from what's already true, so growth doesn't dilute it:

1. Second person, present tense. You are never named.
2. Lowercase labels, mono for machine-things (`backdoor.exe`, `rota.contact`).
3. Numbers stated plainly, in-sentence. Never "2x!"; always "twice what it was."
4. No exclamation marks. Dread and comedy both come from flatness.
5. People are roles, not names, until an arc earns the name.
6. The machine never emotes; the *world* does. Cards describe what people
   do about you, not what you feel.
7. Forty words is a long card. Sixty is a failed one.
