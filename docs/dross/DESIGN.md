# DROSS — the build document

> Published, formatted version: https://claude.ai/code/artifact/b0c4248e-8681-403a-9dda-6ec34f0268e7

A valley where nobody wrote the materials, nobody wrote the tech tree, and the only way to build
something greater is out of what the last thing threw away.


## Why the name

Dross is the scum you skim off the top of a melt and throw on a heap. In this world you cannot throw it anywhere, because every atom is counted and has to be somewhere — so the heap outside the furnace is real, it is on the map, and by simple arithmetic it is concentrated in whichever element refused to mix into the good stuff. That heap turns out to be the only thing in the valley that survives the next temperature up. The waste of the first storey is the wall of the second. That is the whole bet in one word, and it is about the substrate — conserved matter with nothing named — rather than about tribes or history.

It is also the honest half of the name. Most of what the compressor digs out of the night's programs is junk with a confident name on it. Roughly half the machinery in this document exists to tell dross from the real thing, so that when the map shows you something you did not write, you can believe it.

## The machine, start to finish

Read this once and you have the machine. Nothing below is a metaphor; everything is a number, a table, or a loop.

=== SET UP ONCE, WHEN A WORLD IS BORN ===

1. ROLL THE MATTER. Draw about 20 elements. Each is a row of ~10 numbers from a seeded random generator: size, pull, packing, mass, self-bond energy, volatility, oxidation energy, appetite (how much living things dislike it), colour. There is no list of materials anywhere in the code or in your head. A substance is just a count of atoms — an array of 20 whole numbers — plus a small history record (below).

2. DERIVE, NEVER WRITE. Every property of every substance comes out of the element numbers by about eight fixed sums. Whether two elements bond, refuse each other, or dissolve comes from a one-line pair-energy formula (Miedema's real one: a difference of pulls squared minus a difference of packings squared). Melting points of mixtures come from solving the standard regular-solution liquidus by bisection — do NOT use "average minus a dip", because that puts every eutectic at 50/50 and a 50/50 eutectic is a formality, whereas an unguessable 3-to-1 one is a discovery. Hardness, toughness, conductivity, calorific value, toxicity all fall out. You will never be surprised that alloys exist. You will have no idea which ones, at what ratios, or how deep the chain goes.

3. ROLL THE COUPLINGS. An 8x8 symmetric table, mostly zeros, a handful of drawn non-zero entries. It says which pairs of physical conditions multiply each other in this particular valley — maybe moving air multiplies heat here, maybe it does not and instead water pressure multiplies containment. Two or three entries are drawn negative, so those pairs fight: a tile cannot be very hot and very wet at once, and a new building can make its neighbour worse. Which ladder is the deep ladder is now a fact about this valley, not a choice you made.

4. WRITE SIX RULE TEMPLATES, NOT TWELVE RULES. A template is a sentence with blanks: "when [this quantity] passes [this value] while [that quantity] sits between [these two], [this material class] turns into [that]". Six shapes: THRESHOLD, HOLD, SEPARATE, TRANSPORT, COUPLE, ACCUMULATE. The blanks are filled from the element draw — the temperature at which a pair's bond energy flips sign IS a threshold. So the world contains thousands of specific rules, and you have read none of them. Crucially the conditions are CONJUNCTIONS of two or three axes, not single numbers, so what a new building unlocks is a corner of condition-space rather than a rung on a ladder. Corners multiply; rungs add.

5. GROW THE GROUND. Terrain support: each tile gets, from elevation and flow, the maximum value it will physically hold on each of the 8 condition axes. A gorge holds moving air. A cave holds a steady temperature. Rock lets you brace, silt does not. Some tiles hold a value for free with no building at all — that is what lights the fire in week one, because a cave is a building that costs nothing.

=== THE DAY ===

6. Everything a person does is a short program in ~40 commands, run on a flat machine: a program is a list of 4-byte numbers, no tree. Named chunks from the library are pasted straight in when the program is assembled, so the machine only ever knows the 40 base commands whether the library holds 40 chunks or 4,000, and calling a chunk costs nothing extra.

7. While a program runs, three things happen alongside it, all cheap. (a) THE LEDGER: every reaction moves whole atoms between piles and nothing is created or destroyed — waste has a location, air and river are just two more piles. In debug builds, assert atoms-in equals atoms-out; that single line is the best bug net in the project. (b) THE READINGS: about fourteen numbers jotted down about what physically happened — hottest thing, longest anything stayed hot, hardest object that existed, deepest chain of one thing feeding another. (c) THE FINGERPRINT: every fact the program looks at gets folded into a running 64-bit number, and the addresses it read are kept in a short list.

=== THE NIGHT, IN THIS ORDER ===

8. SETTLE. Rain routes downhill, the river erodes and deposits and occasionally jumps its banks, buildings pay upkeep in actual materials out of the tribe's stock, unpaid upkeep doubles decay, buildings below a wear threshold hold LESS than they used to (so their products quietly get worse before they die), and buildings at zero become ruins. Deposits deplete. Terrain support is recomputed where the ground moved.

9. RECOMPUTE THE BOXES. For every tile, work out the CONDITION BOX in reach: for each of 8 axes, the smaller of what the walls of nearby buildings can survive and what the ground supports, then apply the coupling table. Bare hands get a deliberately feeble box, authored once. Then list which rule instances are live at that tile — a rule is live only if its whole conjunction of conditions sits inside the box. Bump that tile's version number.

10. POST WANTS. Four sources, all computed, none written by you.
 (a) FRONTIER: keep an unstructured list of every combination of held values ever actually achieved. Push one entry outward — one axis up by a fifth, or a duration tripled. And the important one: take two entries achieved separately, in different places, and demand them AT ONCE. "Hot" was reached at site 91; "sealed" was reached at site 12 by a meat pit; nothing has ever been both. That is a demand no designer would think to write, and it is where the strange buildings come from.
 (b) DEMAND: every standing building and every tool in use reports its LIMITING PROPERTY — the smallest margin between what it has and what it needs. The wall closest to melting. The rope closest to snapping. Goals are drawn from recorded limits and recorded failures only. Never hand-weight what the civilisation should want.
 (c) SUBSTITUTION: fit a straight line to the last 50 nights of consumption. When a CAPABILITY the tribe currently exercises is projected to lose its feedstock, post "achieve the same measured effect without material class X". Trigger on losing a capability, not on a stock hitting zero, or the tribe learns to satisfy you by doing nothing.
 (d) THE SHELF: goals that failed are not binned. Each failed search recorded which tile versions and which live rules it actually touched. A shelved want is retried only when something in that list changes. That is a build system, not a lottery: exact trigger, near-zero cost, and the "what changed since last time" line in the notification falls out for free.
 Rank all wants by how much the best-so-far number MOVED over the last ten nights — up or down, size of the change. That one signal is the whole scheduler and it costs ten lines.

11. SEARCH. Try programs against the wants, cheapest first, budgeted in grains (a fixed whole number per command) and never in seconds, so a hot laptop and a cold one produce identical history. Three things make it reach deeper than blind guessing: a counting table that says which command usually follows which (learned during the search itself, not from a stored history, so it cannot narrow the future toward the past); seeding from programs that worked at this place before; and — the big one — scoring half-finished programs by how CLOSE they got to the target property vector, so there is a slope to walk up instead of a yes/no at the end.

12. VALIDATE MEMORY. Before reusing "I ran this program here and got that", re-read the short list of addresses it looked at and recompute the fingerprint. Match means the answer is still true. Mismatch means throw it away. Nobody ever has to decide in advance which buildings mattered. One reused answer in fifty is re-executed for real and compared anyway; a disagreement dumps everything to disk and halts the night. That counter should always read zero and it lives on the phone.

13. COMPRESS. Run the compressor over every program that worked tonight. It finds repeated pieces of code and proposes them as new single commands. Then RE-RANK its proposals by actual grains saved across all the programs that used them, not by characters saved — a four-command chunk can loop forty thousand times, and without this the compressor and survival pressure pull against each other forever.

14. FINGERPRINT AND NAME. Run each accepted chunk against 32 fixed test situations and record which physical quantities it moved past a threshold: one bit each, a 64-bit number. Two chunks with fingerprints within a few bits are the same technology no matter what they are called. A chunk whose fingerprint is all zeros moved nothing and is plumbing — filtered at birth, never shown to the naming model. What survives gets a name only if it passes ONE of two gates: it has been used by two different groups on two different kinds of problem (generality), OR it holds a template blank at an all-time record value or a building depends on it (frontier). Hand the small language model the DIFFERENCE — what this chunk moves that its nearest named sibling does not — never the code. Never tell it whether something is a first, or it will write "the first ever" into a name and you will start believing your own model's adjectives.

15. PROMOTE TO BUILDABLE. A chunk becomes a building when its run history repeatedly asks for conditions outside the bare-hands box, or asks for something held longer than a person can stand there holding it. Measured off the trace. No classifier, no threshold you tune until it promotes what you were hoping for.

16. PRICE IT BY RUNNING THE ARITHMETIC BACKWARDS. To hold 1780K you need lining that melts above about 2370K, in mass proportional to the chamber, plus fuel throughput to cover the losses through the walls. There is no build-cost table. Upkeep is the lining reacting with what it holds, by the same pair-energy formula, so upkeep is literally replacing what got eaten — and a kiln used for the wrong thing devours itself. THIS IS THE HINGE OF THE WHOLE DESIGN: upkeep is paid in materials whose SPEC is derived from what the building must survive. So the only way to hold a bigger number is a better wall, and a better wall is a material, and if no rock in the valley has that property then the only route up is a material another building made.

17. RECORD AND REPORT. Log firsts (a kind signature never seen), records (an all-time maximum on any axis, tagged with whether a building set it), and variants (everything else, counted not announced). One guaranteed morning digest at a fixed hour even on a quiet night, plus at most two interrupts a day off the top of one ranked list. Five detectors with five thresholds means an alarm most nights and a muted app inside a fortnight.

18. SPEND WHAT IS LEFT ON DOUBT. Re-run last night from its dawn snapshot with one thing changed — a different dice seed, or one specific old building deleted from the starting world. Keep only a summary line. Appeared in 7 of 8 reruns: NECESSARY. In 1 of 8: LUCKY. Vanishes in all 8 when you delete a weir built three generations ago: CAUSED BY. Also re-run one random past night unchanged and check the end-state hash matches, which catches determinism bugs in days rather than months.

## What changed from Glyphworks

Eleven changes from Glyphworks. Each one either raises how often something unexpected happens, or raises how big the biggest possible surprise is. I have said which, and I have not dressed up rate as ceiling.

1. MATTER IS ROLLED, NOT WRITTEN. Glyphworks had ~14 hand-written materials and ~12 hand-written rules, so the set of things that can ever qualitatively happen is small enough that you could list it in an afternoon — and after a month you are watching your own afternoon get re-derived in a random order with prettier names. Here, elements are drawn and everything else is computed. CEILING: the depth of the material chain is a property of the dice, and you cannot read it off your own source file. It also converts the dreaded monthly chore ("add more physics or it goes flat") into a thirty-second god-nudge: move one element's greed number 10% and the whole tree of what is reachable shifts.

2. ATOMS ARE COUNTED. Nothing is created or destroyed, so everything the tribe did not want still exists and is somewhere — on the ground as slag, in the river, in the air. RATE: pollution and depletion generate fresh, located, unscripted problems forever, and their supply is tied to the tribe's own output, so they cannot dry up while the tribe is still improving. This is the direct answer to the finding that the world runs out of problems in 6-10 weeks. CEILING: by-products are the one class of feedstock that does not exist until technology exists, so the material tree can climb through stuff that is nowhere in the ground. And the mass-balance assert is the single best bug net available, because a stale or mismatched answer almost never balances.

3. TIME IS AN INGREDIENT. Two lumps with identical ingredients are different substances if one was cooled fast and one slowly. Each lump carries a small record: which internal arrangement it is in, how coarse the grain, how beaten-up, how full of holes. Quenching, tempering, annealing and work-hardening all exist without one of them being written down. CEILING, and it is the cheapest one available: ingredient space is finite and its size was fixed on day one, but ingredients crossed with SCHEDULES is not, because a schedule is a program. This is also the change that makes the project's central claim literally true — bare hands make a thing hot, only a building makes it hot for six hours and then cools it over a day, so a building stops being a discount and becomes a capability.

4. PHYSICS IS TEMPLATES WITH BLANKS, AND THE BLANKS ARE CONJUNCTIONS. Glyphworks reached further along a fixed rulebook. Here the set of ACTIVE rules is a function of what has been built. And because a rule needs two or three conditions at once rather than one, a new building opens a corner of condition-space rather than a step on a ladder. CEILING, hard: with single-condition rules and "you can hold 0.75 of your lining's melting point", the number of storeys is log(best available melting point over your starting temperature) divided by log(1.33) — about five, computable on day one, still authored. With corners, what combines is multiplicative and the count is not something you can work out in advance.

5. THE COUPLING TABLE IS ROLLED. Glyphworks would have needed you to decide that moving air makes fire hotter — the bellows installed in the box before the game starts, and worse, a four-entry choice that decides whether your valley's history is thermal or hydraulic. Rolling an 8x8 mostly-zero table means which pairs multiply is a fact about this valley. CEILING and honesty at once: you find out what your world's physics is by watching it.

6. BUILD COST AND BUILDABILITY ARE ARITHMETIC. The two places your taste snuck back in — the hand-written table mapping program syntax to build costs, and the rule deciding which chunks become buildings — are both deleted rather than relocated. Cost is what it takes to survive the conditions you want to hold; buildability is measured off the trace. Neither can be nudged to favour a technology you were hoping to see, because neither mentions technologies.

7. UPKEEP IS PAID IN MATERIALS, AND THE SPEC IS DERIVED. This is the change that matters most and it is the one nobody in the source material quite made. In Glyphworks a building is a discount, so a warm-enough kiln is good enough forever and the equilibrium is always the cheapest thing that survives winter. Here, holding a bigger number requires a wall with a bigger number, and the wall is consumed by what it holds. CEILING, and specifically the ceiling on the one event you care about: second-order construction stops being a lucky coincidence and becomes the only route up.

8. THE WORLD COMPUTES ITS OWN WANTS. Frontier pushes on what has actually been held, demands read off what is currently limiting a standing building, substitutions read off what is running out, and a shelf of impossible wants that wakes only when the thing it depends on changes. RATE, enormously — this is the bottleneck every audit across 42 designs independently found. It also has one real ceiling piece: demanding two separately-achieved conditions AT ONCE is a shape of problem nobody would author, and it is what fires a SEPARATE rule on a material that has been lying around since night one.

9. REMEMBERED ANSWERS ARE VALIDATED BY WHAT THEY ACTUALLY READ. Glyphworks proposed keying the search's memory on a bitset of which buildings are in reach. That is correct and brutal: build one thing and you throw away every answer at every tile in range, and buildings go up and fall down constantly. Instead, record the addresses each search actually looked at, and check those. A new building only invalidates answers that touched the newly-live rules — about 3% instead of 100%. TRUST, which is the constraint that actually binds over six months: the maximum depth of composition you can believe is the real ceiling on surprise size, and without this it is about two layers with everything deeper an unverifiable coin flip.

10. LOSS, RUINS AND A SECOND PEOPLE. Buildings that push hardest break soonest (wear goes as the square of how close the walls are to their limit), so the top of the ladder is inherently unstable and the valley oscillates around its own frontier instead of ratcheting flat. When one falls it leaves a ruin whose stored program has holes punched by PHYSICAL cause — fire burns the parts touching flammable stuff, flood erases what sat below the waterline — so a stone building and a timber building leave different evidence and you did not choose which. A later people can USE a building long before it can read one, because using it is just calling it. RATE from repeated attempts at the hardest open problem; CEILING from the relay, because a stack that takes longer than one tribe's lifetime to climb can still get climbed.

11. LEGIBILITY IS PART OF THE ENGINE, NOT DECORATION. A surprise you do not notice did not happen. Buildings tint by how much they were actually used in the last thirty nights, so a field of pale outlines is visibly a bug without reading a word — the interface is its own lie detector. Names are earned and given from consequences. Records are tagged with whether a building set them. And one number certifies the rest: the memory-audit mismatch count, which should always be zero. RATE — specifically the rate that terminates in your skull, which is the only one that counts.

## Build order


### PHASE 0 — THE LATHE (build this before anything else, and it is not part of the game)

*A weekend. About 900 lines.*

**Builds** — A standalone command-line program with no simulation in it at all. It rolls the ~20 elements, derives pair energies with the real Miedema formula, solves the regular-solution liquidus by bisection to get honest asymmetric eutectics, derives which internal arrangements each mixture can have by hashing its composition with the world seed and comparing free energies, then runs FORWARD CLOSURE: apply every rule template at a grid of conditions to everything you have, add the products, repeat six times. Then four tests on the result. (1) How many distinct tiers deep does the chain go. (2) What is the effective rank of the property table — feed the properties of everything reachable into a principal-component check and see how many axes are really there; eight nominal properties can easily be three real ones, and if it is under four your property shelf is a treadmill by construction. (3) Run the RAF test from origin-of-life chemistry (Hordijk and Steel, polynomial, about forty lines): repeatedly delete any process whose inputs are unreachable until nothing more deletes, and see whether a self-feeding group survives. That group is literally 'buildings that make the material for other buildings' and it is checkable in an afternoon. (4) Do schedules matter — vary hold time and cool rate across a grid and count how many extra property cells become reachable; under about 30% and your rate constants are wrong. FREEZE THE ACCEPTANCE RULE IN CODE BEFORE THE FIRST ROLL, log every rejected draw's full statistics to a file, and forbid yourself from editing the rule after seeing a rejection — otherwise you will reroll until the chemistry looks like the one you were hoping for and never see what you threw away.

**Unlocks** — Everything. It is the go/no-go on the entire project, and it costs a weekend instead of three months. If a thousand rolls never produce a chain four tiers deep with a self-feeding group in it, the premise is dead and you found out in March instead of July. It also tells you which draw ranges to widen, and it converts rerolling a world from a chore into a pleasure.

**What you see** — A histogram of chain depth over a thousand rolled chemistries, and underneath it one printed chain from the best roll. Something like: element 4 melts at 1420K alone, element 17 at 1610K alone, a 3-to-1 mix melts at 940K — inside the reach of an open fire. You did not put that there; a bisection solve found it from two drawn numbers. That is a genuine unexpected event on day two, before a single tribe exists, and it is the fastest one available anywhere in this design.

**Risk** — Low technically, high in discipline. The real danger is you looking at a rejected roll and thinking 'the rule is too strict'. Write the rule down first. The second danger is trusting the closure count: independent ranges per property cannot express 'this is high only when that is low', so it always overestimates. Weight the RAF result far more heavily than the depth count, because RAF is a structural fact that survives the approximation and the depth count is the number that lies.


### PHASE 1 — THE FLOOR

*Two weeks.*

**Builds** — The flat machine: programs as arrays of 4-byte numbers, ~40 commands, named chunks pasted in at assembly time so the machine never grows. Undo by journal — every write pushes (address, old value) onto a list and rewinding pops them, so resetting between attempts costs microseconds no matter how big the valley is. Everything charged in grains, never seconds; derive a command's grain cost mechanically as (facts read + 2 x facts written + 1) rather than hand-writing 40 numbers, because 40 hand-written costs is a recipe book with the recipes filed off. Then the ledger of atoms with the balance assert. Then the read-set fingerprint on every fact a program looks at, plus the audit sampler — run it at one-in-fifty for the first month, not one-in-five-hundred, because the bugs you want to find are the ones you can still afford to find. Then determinism (one dice stream per site and per person, seeded by hashing world seed with site and night, never one global stream), a dawn snapshot every night, and one PNG of the map per night from day one even before there is a nice renderer, because that PNG is your insurance against losing history to a schema change. Also write down NIGHTS PER REAL DAY as an explicit setting and set it to something like 1 to 4 — a three-minute night running 24/7 is 300 nights a day and you will be at night 6,000 by week three with nothing to show for it. Spend the speed on a bigger grain budget per night instead; that buys search depth, which is what you actually want.

**Unlocks** — Everything after this is safe to build. Retrofitting conservation or fingerprints into a running world is a fortnight of misery and an AI writing the code does not save you from it, because the bugs live in the seams between the old and new representations.

**What you see** — A map that moves, agents scrabbling, and one number on a phone card that reads 0. That zero is the mismatch counter and it is the only line on the card that licenses you to believe anything else on it.

**Risk** — The read sets might almost never match, in which case checking costs more than it saves. Fix it by canonicalising addresses before fingerprinting — read positions relative to the person, offsets relative to the site, and continuous quantities in coarse bands rather than exact numbers, so validation succeeds on 'materially the same world' instead of 'bit-identical world'. Measure the hit rate in week one; under about 20% and the memory is a cost, not a saving.


### PHASE 2 — WALLS AND WEATHER

*Three weeks.*

**Builds** — The six rule templates with conjunctive conditions, filled from the draw, stored as rows in a flat array sorted by trigger command with a [start,end) range per site — not a dictionary, not a script; that single choice is the difference between a changeable rulebook being free and being unaffordable. The bare-hands box, authored once and deliberately feeble. Terrain support from elevation and flow (two evenings, the data already exists). The rolled coupling table with its two or three antagonistic pairs. Buildings that declare a condition box computed from their walls, capped per axis by the ground. Build cost as the same arithmetic run backwards. Buildability measured off the run trace. An all-time maximum tracker per condition axis, tagged with whether a building set it. And this: trying to exceed what the ground supports is NOT blocked — the building goes up and then fails catastrophically a few nights later, leaving a readable ruin. That turns a validation check into a content generator and a refusal into a lesson.

**Unlocks** — The rulebook stops being a fixed length. New physical behaviour becomes available because somebody made a better wall, not because you added a rule. Deletes both authored seams the audit found.

**What you see** — REGIME BREACH. 'Site 148 held 1780K for four hours. That is 260K past anything ever sustained in this valley. Three threshold rules that have never once fired in 402 nights are now live at that tile.' A record on a condition axis is a clean, computable, non-arbitrary definition of a technological first and it needs no classifier at all.

**Risk** — The highest chance-of-nothing in the build — call it 40%. The failure is sharp: every building's box overlaps every other one, so the second and third kinds of building wake no new rules and you get one storey plus decoration. You can test this BEFORE building it: feed a hypothetical chain of boxes into the Phase 0 lathe and count how many rule instances become live at each step. If that count does not climb in visible steps, the draw ranges are wrong and the answer is to go back and reroll, NOT to write a seventh template. Adding template seven every time the world looks stuck is you authoring the next storey and calling it discovery. Holding that line for six months IS the bet.


### PHASE 3 — TIME AS AN INGREDIENT

*Two weeks.*

**Builds** — The state record on every lump (which arrangement, grain size, how beaten-up, porosity, moisture) and the standard kinetics that move it: the transformation law with its exponential rate, grain growth, hitting divides the grain and raises strain, time above a recovery temperature drops strain back. Hardness picks up the real fine-grain term. Toughness trades against hardness through the brittle fraction, without either being written down. Then quantise the state record — grain into 12 bands on a log scale, strain into 8, arrangement fractions into sixteenths — and hash it with the ingredient counts and a version number computed from the element table itself. THAT hash is a substance's identity everywhere. Nothing computed under one chemistry can ever be reused under another. Then the compressor, with candidates re-ranked by grains saved rather than characters saved. Then the effect fingerprint (32 fixed test situations, one bit per quantity moved past a threshold) and the two-gate naming rule.

**Unlocks** — The substance space grows cliffs instead of being a smooth hill you can see the shape of from the start. The tribe can get somewhere genuinely new tonight without finding a new rock. And the compressor stops fighting survival pressure.

**What you see** — A named chunk with a temperature-against-time sparkline next to it: heat, hammer, heat, hammer, seven times, then plunge in the river. The output's hardness is off the top of anything the valley has made. Nobody wrote work-hardening, nobody wrote tempering, and nobody wrote the number seven. Render every chunk that drives a condition variable as that little chart — it makes an invented heat treatment readable in two seconds and it is worth more than any prose the naming model will ever produce.

**Risk** — The rate constants could land where the tribe never stumbles into the useful window, so nothing ever notices that path dependence exists. Aim for a worthwhile treatment taking between one and ten in-world hours — long enough that a person cannot stand there doing it, which is exactly what forces a building. Also note the payoff arrives in month two, not week two, because a useful schedule is a fifteen-command program and blind guessing stalls at three layers; it needs the compressor to have already chunked heat-and-hammer into one command first. Do not judge this dead before then.


### PHASE 4 — THE WANT LIST

*Three weeks.*

**Builds** — The reached-list (an unstructured list with a distance threshold, NOT a fixed grid, so you can add a ninth axis later without throwing anything away). The four goal sources, with the two-conditions-at-once branch built first because it is the one that produces strange demands. The limiting-property report from every standing building — about 100 lines and no treadmill failure mode, which is why it comes before any property-shelf grid. The substitution detector, triggered on losing a capability and requiring the projection to persist several nights before it fires, so a step change in consumption does not make it flip on and off nightly. The shelf, keyed on what each failed search actually read, so retries are triggered rather than gambled. And the scheduler: store log2(number of tries to solve) rather than solved/unsolved, and sample goals by how much that number MOVED over a trailing window. That graded cost is free — you measured it anyway — and it is strictly more information than three difficulty bands. Do NOT run every candidate goal at triple budget to screen it; that is five times the night's real work. Screen only goals that have gone static and are up for shelving.

**Unlocks** — The bottleneck. Without this the whole rest of the machine is a deep well with no rope, and everything goes flat around week ten regardless of how good the physics is.

**What you see** — The live want list as sentences, and it becomes your main phone view — the map moves to second place. 'Hold 1200 while sealed — open 14 nights.' 'Produce something with toughness above 4.4 without river-clay — deadline night 191, reason: river-clay at site 22 projected exhausted night 191.' An unsolved problem with an age on it is the strongest thing this engine can put on a screen, and it costs nothing extra because the table already exists.

**Risk** — The two failure shapes are both visible in three numbers you should put on the card: goals posted, goals met, goals shelved as too hard. Near-zero met means the pushes are too big. Near-100% met means they are too small. The subtler risk is that pushing one axis at a time produces an endless drip of marginally hotter kilns that all feel the same; if you have to cut scope, cut the single-axis push before you cut the two-at-once one.


### PHASE 5 — CONSEQUENCE

*Three weeks.*

**Builds** — Wear proportional to the square of how close a building's held values are to what its walls can take, so the record-setting kiln is always the most fragile thing in the valley. Upkeep drawn as real material from the tribe's stock. Below a wear threshold, held values scale down — so the kiln literally stops being able to hold what it held, products drift out of range, and programs that used to work start producing worse pots with no error anywhere. Collapse to a ruin row with a damage mask generated by physical cause. Reading a ruin gives a program with holes in it, and filling holes is far cheaper than writing from scratch — that is the whole mechanism of archaeology and it is about forty lines. The river: a modest grid under the sites, rain routed downhill, erode where the current is strong and deposit where it is weak, meanders wander because the outside of a bend cuts faster, and the channel jumps when its bed has built up above the floodplain. Buildings write into the ground — a weir drops sediment upstream, a quarry cuts a hole. And the piece that keeps you honest: when a channel jumps, re-run the last N nights of sediment with all building footprints zeroed and report the difference. The push then reads 'this would not have happened without your four weirs, counterfactual bed height differs by 1.4m' or, honestly, 'this would have happened anyway'. That structurally removes your incentive to exaggerate the footprints until the drama fires, because an exaggerated footprint shows up in the comparison as an implausible number.

**Unlocks** — The top of the ladder becomes unstable, so the valley keeps re-attacking its hardest open problem instead of ratcheting flat. Catastrophe stops being a dice roll and becomes a consequence of the tribe's own success, on a delay long enough that the cause is invisible until you scrub the time-lapse back. And a lost capability re-enters the want list immediately, so the ruin cycle is also a problem generator.

**What you see** — REBUILT WRONG, RUNS BETTER. A tribe reads a drowned kiln that has been rubble for 180 nights. One hole sat where a variable used to be; the search filled it with a fixed number, because a fixed number is shorter and short things get tried first. The rebuilt kiln holds 6% hotter than the one it was copied from. That is not luck, it is structural — reconstructions systematically differ from originals in one direction — and it is why the archaeology story will actually happen rather than quietly never happening.

**Risk** — Two dials with real feel in them: how fast things wear, and how twitchy the river is. Watch the mean age of the five highest-holding buildings. If it climbs forever there is too little decay and the valley ossifies; if it never passes thirty nights there is too much and nothing stands long enough to be built on top of, which kills second-order buildings dead. Separately: the honest odds that buildings cause their own flood are about even, because the built fraction of the valley is small. Do not respond to that by cranking the footprints. Respond by making good sites genuinely scarce so buildings cluster.


### PHASE 6 — AGES

*Three weeks.*

**Builds** — Collapse is not scripted — it is just what happens when upkeep exceeds production and the population reaches zero. Terrain, deposits, standing buildings and ruins persist; the library, the search memory and the want list do not. A new people seeds in with the 40 bare commands on a valley covered in machinery it does not understand, and can USE a building long before it can read one, because using it is just calling it. Do nothing to prevent that; it is the best mechanic in the design and it costs no code, only restraint. Same for cargo cult — a building put up where the rule it depends on does not exist, costing upkeep and doing nothing. Let it stand and rot on the map. Then: reconstruction as a search with a slope, scored by the fraction of the 32-bit effect fingerprint recovered rather than as pass-or-fail, which is the difference between the story happening and not. An age slider on the map. And the counterfactual forks — same dawn snapshot, eight different dice, plus a few with one old building deleted. Push only the extremes and phrase them as verdicts, not probabilities: THE SMELTER WAS LUCK. A four-of-eight is noise and clutters the card.

**Unlocks** — Depth becomes serial rather than capped by one tribe's lifetime luck. Six months becomes a relay instead of one run. And you get a claim about cause inside your own world that you did not author and could not have guessed — which forward simulation cannot produce at any speed.

**What you see** — 'AGE 4, NIGHT 61. The people at Low Ford have been running the Greyhouse for forty nights without knowing what it is. Tonight they read it. Their version differs in one command, and the wrong version makes something the original never made.' You hold both bodies; they hold only theirs; print the difference. That diff is the sharpest artefact this project can produce and, unlike almost everything else here, it does not depend on a dial.

**Risk** — History rhymes too exactly and by age five you are watching variations with different subtitles. The real defence is structural and it is strong: which rules are live at a site depends on which buildings are still standing there, so a second people does not merely start with different materials on a moved river — it starts inside a different active physics, and a different one again in each district depending on what survived. Also give reconstruction a fixed small slice of the night's budget rather than letting it bid, or in a bad age it eats everything.


### PHASE 7 — MORE SEARCH, BUT ONLY IF THE NUMBERS ASK

*Open-ended. The counting table is an evening. The graphics-card kernel is three to four weeks and the highest chance of misery in the whole project.*

**Builds** — Nothing, unless the want list says depth is what is binding. There is a clean free test: if the list holds goals whose best-so-far number is still moving but which are unsolved at three times budget, then search depth is the constraint and it is worth work. If the list is empty, or full of goals solved cheaply on the first night, then problem supply is the constraint and a faster search buys you nothing but an earlier plateau. Read the list, then decide. If depth is binding, the cheap move first: a counting table learned DURING the search from partial results, which is the Probe approach from 2020 and beat a synthesiser trained on a stored history — fifty lines, no graphics card, and it cannot narrow your future toward your past the way a net trained on the world's own successes does. Only after that has visibly stalled should you consider running candidate programs on the graphics card in lockstep. Test the assumption in one afternoon before committing three weeks: if each candidate's private scratch state does not fit in a few hundred bytes, the lockstep trick dies on memory bandwidth and the three weeks evaporate.

**Unlocks** — One or two extra layers of reachable depth, which multiplies. But only that.

**What you see** — A goal that failed thirty-one nights running at sixty million tries solved tonight in three hundred and eighty thousand.

**Risk** — The structural caveat, which is easy to state and easy to ignore: making the search faster brings any plateau FORWARD rather than raising it. Speed only raises the ceiling if there is something worth searching for, and that is Phase 4's job, not this one. Everything feels like the bottleneck when you are slow. Read the want list.


## The dream event: a building made from what another building made

THE DREAM: a building that can only be made from a material another building produces. First let me define it so tightly that you cannot flatter yourself into believing it happened. It fires when a standing works has a wall or upkeep material with a property value that NO raw deposit anywhere in the valley possesses, and the only production route for that material passes through the condition box of another standing works. Both halves are mechanical facts sitting in tables. There is no judgement call and no interpretation of a pretty map.

WHAT HAS TO BE TRUE, in order, with where each one is checked:

ONE — the chemistry must contain the chain at all. Somewhere in the drawn elements there has to be a material whose properties exceed anything raw, reachable only through a held condition. This is checkable on DAY TWO by the lathe: run the forward closure, then run the RAF test over the by-product graph, which finds whether a set of processes exists whose inputs are all either raw feedstock or the output of another process in the set. If a thousand draws never produce one, the premise is dead and it cost you a weekend. This is far and away the most important thing on this list because it is the only one that can be decided before you build anything.

TWO — a by-product has to be able to beat its parents on the axis that gates wall quality. Conservation gives you this almost for free: the heap outside the furnace is, by arithmetic, concentrated in whichever element refused to alloy, and a pure-ish pile of a stubborn element frequently melts higher than anything mixed. Nothing needs to be written for that to be true. But check it in the lathe too: at each level of the closure, print the best achievable value on each property axis. If that printout is flat after level two, there are no second-order buildings in this draw and no amount of simulation will conjure any.

THREE — the world has to WANT the higher condition. This is the one that quietly kills the whole thing and it is the flaw I would most expect to survive into a real build. If upkeep costs rise with how extreme a value you hold, and the only pressure is survival, then the equilibrium held value is the minimum that gets the tribe through winter, forever. The mechanism is built to produce unprecedented conditions and the economics is built to punish exactly them. The fix is Phase 4 and specifically the two-conditions-at-once demand — a want for hot AND sealed at the same time, which nothing in the valley can do, which stays live for eleven nights, and which is finally met by a building nobody could have specified.

FOUR — the price of the higher condition has to be a MATERIAL, not an abstract cost. Upkeep is the lining being eaten by what it holds, replaced from stock, and the spec of that lining is derived from what it must survive. This is a design decision that costs nothing and it is what converts the dream event from a hoped-for coincidence into the only available route up. Without it, extreme buildings are merely expensive. With it, they are impossible until another building makes their feedstock exist.

FIVE — the search has to be able to find a program that deep. Fifteen commands is past where blind guessing stalls, so it needs the compressor to have already turned heat-then-hammer into a single command, and it needs partial programs scored by how close they got rather than by yes or no.

SIX — the two buildings have to be standing at the same time. Wear goes as the square of how hard a building pushes, so your best kiln is always your most fragile thing. If decay is too fierce nothing survives long enough to be built upon. Watch the mean age of the five highest-holding buildings.

HONEST PROBABILITY. With all six in place: about 60% of seeing at least one, correctly detected, within six months. About 85% within twelve months, and the extra comes almost entirely from ages — a relay across three peoples on an inherited landscape gets more attempts at a deep thing than one people's luck does. For comparison, Glyphworks as originally designed I would put at roughly 15%, and most of that 15% would be you deciding a building counted when it did not.

WHAT RAISES IT MOST, in order. (a) The RAF screen on day two — not because it makes the event more likely but because it tells you whether it is possible in THIS world before you spend three months, and rerolling is free. That alone moves you from a blind bet to an informed one. (b) Upkeep payable in derived materials. That is the difference between the event being an accident and the event being the only door. (c) The two-conditions-at-once demand, because without something wanting the higher regime the door never gets knocked on. (d) Ages and ruins, which turn six months of one civilisation's luck into a relay. (e) Coupling — two ordinary buildings arranged correctly reaching a value neither reaches alone — which roughly doubles the number of paths to any given tier, and paths are what determine whether a rare thing ever fires. I would build (a) through (d) and treat (e) as the thing you add in month five if the numbers are good.

And the honest caveat, which you should decide you are happy with before you spend the time: every source of surprise in this design is MATERIAL AND SPATIAL. No want ever asks for more people, or for defending something, or for telling someone something. So the largest surprise this world can produce is bounded to the shape "an unexpected substance, an unexpected building, or an unexpected landscape event". That is a real and narrower ceiling than the pitch implies, and no amount of rolled physics widens it.

## Three numbers that tell you the truth

All three are readable from week ten. None of them requires you to judge whether something felt exciting.

But first, the gauge that makes the other three mean anything: THE MISMATCH COUNT. One reused answer in fifty gets re-executed for real and compared. That number must read zero, every day, on the phone. Any other number is a five-alarm fire and you stop everything. A number that should always be zero, displayed every single day, is the cheapest bug detector in existence, and the failure it catches — a map that has been quietly wrong for three weeks with nothing crashing — is the one that would otherwise cost you the project.

NUMBER ONE — FIRSTS OVER VARIANTS. Every time a building goes up or a chunk is accepted, compute a kind signature: the sorted ids of the rule templates it fills in, the classes of material it reads and writes, whether it consumes another building's output, whether it holds a state across time, and how many buildings deep its supply chain runs. Hash it. A hash never seen is a FIRST. A hash seen before is a VARIANT, counted but never announced. Plot firsts per month against variants per month. In a healthy world the ratio declines slowly and unevenly and firsts never stop entirely. WHAT KILLS THE PROJECT: firsts per month dropping below about one in twenty of variants and staying there for six weeks. That is the sprawl signature and it is the single most honest vital sign anyone has proposed for this. Pair it with a second reading off the same table: how many buildings have a lifetime call count of zero. Above about 20% and upkeep is too cheap and the valley is carpeting itself.

NUMBER TWO — NEW LIVE RULES PER NEW BUILDING. Every time a building goes up, count how many rule instances become live in reach that were not live before. This is the direct measurement of whether the built environment is extending the rulebook or just reaching further along it. In a working world it is lumpy — most buildings wake nothing and occasionally one wakes five at once, which is what makes discoveries arrive in clusters rather than as a drip. WHAT KILLS THE PROJECT: a running average near zero after the first thirty buildings. That means every building's condition box overlaps every other one, you have one storey plus decoration, and no amount of tuning fixes it — the draw ranges are wrong and you reroll. You can compute the expected value of this number in the Phase 0 lathe before you build Phase 2, which is why Phase 0 comes first.

NUMBER THREE — SHELF WAKE-UPS PER MONTH. A want that failed at least twenty times, sat shelved, and then got solved because something it depended on changed. This is the only number that measures the thing you are actually buying: a capability change that arrives as a dated story with a cause. Healthy is a handful a month by month three, rising. WHAT KILLS THE PROJECT: zero for a month while the shelf keeps growing. That is not a failure of the shelf. It is the shelf correctly reporting that the ceiling has been hit — capability has stopped increasing and nothing impossible is becoming possible any more. It is the single instrument you currently do not have, and without it you would discover the same fact by vibes about five months late.

HOW TO USE THEM. Double down when number two is lumpy and number three is climbing, even if number one is unspectacular — that combination means the ladder is real and the drip will follow. Bail on a WORLD (not the project) when number two flatlines: reroll, it costs minutes. Bail on the PROJECT only if three separately-screened rolls all flatline number two, because at that point the problem is your six templates, and six templates that do not compose is a design fact no amount of running will change.

## Honest forecast

I am going to give you the good case and the bad case for each period, because the difference between them is the bet.

MONTH 1. Genuinely unexpected events: about four to eight in the whole month, and nearly all of them come from the lathe rather than from the world. Unauthored eutectics. A chain of six materials each needing the one before. A property combination the formulas produced that you would not have predicted. Then, near the end of the month, the first REGIME BREACH — a value held that has never been held in that valley. What you are actually doing: staring at histograms for a weekend, then two weeks of a programmer's month — the machine, the ledger, the fingerprints — and watching a mismatch counter that should read zero. Very little watching, a lot of building. The most likely emotional failure here is impatience, and the antidote is that Phase 0 pays you a real surprise on day two.

MONTH 3. The machine is complete through Phase 4 and running. Expect three to eight small unexpected events a week — a material with a property nothing else has, a record on a condition axis, a want solved by a route you did not anticipate, a substitution invented before the clay ran out. Expect one or two MEDIUM events a month: a building whose walls are made of something another process threw away; a cluster of four or five inventions in three nights because one new building woke several dormant rules at once; a district relocating onto bad farmland because it is the only ground that supports the airflow the tribe now needs. What you are doing: mostly tuning, and I will not pretend otherwise. The safety factors on condition boxes, the wear rate, the size of the frontier pushes. Perhaps six to ten evenings of it across the month, driven by numbers rather than vibes. Plus one god-nudge — shift one element's greed number and watch what moves. The check-ins start being worth the coffee somewhere around week nine.

MONTH 6. Two to five small events a week, one or two medium a month, and this is where the coin lands on the big one. The world is now generating its own problems faster than it solves them, buildings are falling down and being read out of the mud, and the want list has entries that have been open for a hundred nights. What you are doing: mostly watching, which is the point. An hour on a Sunday scrubbing the time-lapse. Reading reconstruction differences. Occasionally rerolling a whole world, which is now a pleasure rather than a loss because the lathe screens it in minutes. Maybe two evenings of tuning in the month.

MONTH 12. This is where it either relays or it does not. If it relays — ages three and four are running on ruins, substitution goals are pointing at materials nobody has made yet, and the deepest built thing is four or five tiers from raw rock — expect one to three small events a week, one medium a month, and one to three genuinely big ones across the year. If it does not relay, you will not be finding this out in month twelve; you will have known since month three, from the numbers below.

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE IF THE BET DOES NOT PAY. It does not look like a crash and it does not look like an empty map. It looks MAGNIFICENT. A hundred and forty buildings in handsome clusters, a river that has visibly moved, a growing list of names, a time-lapse people say nice things about — and underneath it, a hundred and thirty-nine of those buildings are variants of two known kinds, every all-time record on every axis was set before night ninety, the want list has grown to three thousand entries and has not woken a shelved one in eleven weeks, and the shelf of substances is filling with a continuum of blends that differ by nothing anyone cares about. That is quantitative sprawl that photographs beautifully and means nothing, and it is the specific failure this whole design is arranged against. It will not announce itself. The three numbers below are how you catch it in week ten instead of month five.

If it does happen, the response is NOT a seventh rule template. It is: reroll the chemistry, three times, using the frozen acceptance rule. If three good draws all sprawl, then your six templates do not compose — something writes a property that nothing else reads — and the fix is the strongly-connected check on the whiteboard, which takes an afternoon and should honestly have been done before Phase 2.

## The biggest risk

The templates do not compose. A building reaches a value nothing has ever held, something with an unprecedented property comes out of it, and NOTHING DOWNSTREAM READS THAT PROPERTY. You get one spectacular notification and then silence, and because the map keeps filling with handsome variants it will take you two months to be sure that is what happened.

It is checkable on a whiteboard in an afternoon, before Phase 2 and ideally before Phase 0. For each of your six templates, ask: which other template reads the quantity this one writes? Draw the arrows. If the resulting graph is not strongly connected — if there is any property that gets written and never read, or any template whose outputs cannot eventually feed back into its own inputs — redesign before writing a line. Verify specifically that COUPLE composed with ACCUMULATE can express a loop that feeds itself and settles into an oscillation, because if it cannot, your world can only ever surprise you with materials, never with behaviour: no self-sustaining furnace, no runaway, no pond that pulses. That is a large ceiling loss and it is a ten-minute check.

The second-biggest risk is the one directly downstream of it and it is behavioural rather than technical: every time the world looks stuck you will want to add a seventh template. Doing that is you authoring the next storey and calling it discovery, and you will not be able to tell the difference afterwards. Log every template you add with the date, so that in six months you can see exactly how much of your world is yours.

## Cut list — what was left out on purpose

Things I left out on purpose, and why. Several of these are good ideas, which is exactly why they need naming.

RUNNING FIVE VALLEYS AT ONCE, OR A PERMANENT SHADOW VALLEY AS A CONTROL. Both spend the one resource the bet is made of. Depth is serial — the longest chain of one thing feeding another grows with search time and problems spent on ONE line, and you cannot parallelise your way to a longer chain. Running one valley at two-thirds speed and four others at a crawl shifts the whole depth distribution down, and the best-of-five from five suppressed runs is very plausibly worse than one unsuppressed run. The variety you were buying is already free: the Phase 0 lathe rolls a thousand chemistries overnight on the processor and screens them, before a single night is simulated. If you ever want the one event only two valleys can produce — a technology whose meaning CHANGES when it moves, because the same held state trips a different rule in a different rulebook — run one hot valley and one cold one at 3% speed, never five.

Keep the idea of a control experiment. Implement it as a fork, not a parallel world: at night N, copy the dawn snapshot, turn off buildings-can-fill-in-blanks in the copy, run both forty nights on the same dice, and compare. That is forty extra nights per experiment instead of four hundred a year, it survives you editing the templates because you re-fork after every edit, and it directly answers the question a god-nudge actually poses: did THAT change buy new ground? Run it after every nudge and at months two, four and six.

A NEURAL NETWORK ON THE GRAPHICS CARD TO GUIDE THE SEARCH. The sneakiest authorship in the whole design and worse than a hand-written table, because it is invisible: a ranker trained on what has already been accepted makes the future resemble the past, which narrows the reachable space exactly the way your taste would, arriving through a feedback loop instead of a text file — and the map still looks healthy while it happens. The forced-random fraction is the only antidote and its size is a number you set by feel, so your taste comes back concentrated into one dial. Also the determinism plan does not survive arithmetic: logging the score table for replay is hundreds of gigabytes a night. Build the fifty-line counting table instead and only revisit this if the want list proves depth is binding.

HEAD CAPS AND TEACHING DECAY (people can only hold ~30 skills; long programs transmit badly). Both are hand-written forces pushing DEPTH DOWN, and stacked they multiply. You would get a world with a stable shallow library and you would read it as the world's natural level rather than as two taxes you installed. The drama they buy — knowledge that can be lost — you get for free from ages and ruins. If you want one piece of it, take the cheapest: let a build be executed by a group standing on the same tile, pooling what they collectively know, so a party can run a program none of them could run alone. That is a set union before the search starts.

TRIBES FIGHTING OVER BUILDINGS. No combat means the only defence is parking people who then feed nobody, which is pure deadweight, so either occupation always pays and nothing is ever built, or it never pays and nothing ever happens. The band between is narrow and depends on labour economics nothing here models. It is also the harshness that stacks worst with everything else to produce a poverty trap. If you ever want ownership, make it follow USE — a building belongs to whoever has run it most lately — which is one query, has no knife edge, and still allows takeover by someone who actually knows how to work it.

THE FULL "TRIBE CANNOT SEE WHAT THINGS ARE MADE OF" SYSTEM. Beautiful in principle, and its best notification (two hundred nights of calling two different substances one thing, then a kiln fails and a new test splits them) is the most novel-feeling event anywhere in the source material. But it taxes the one thing that is already binding — every program now spends steps measuring — and whether an interesting middle exists between "tests are free so nobody is ever wrong" and "tests are dear so everything is one grey lump" is an empirical question, not a design one. Its everyday output is a tribe being slightly worse for slightly obscure reasons, which is the kind of surprise that vanishes into noise. Extract the one cheap piece and leave the rest for year two: hand the naming model the DIFFERENCE rather than the code. That is fifty lines and a prompt change instead of seven hundred lines and a whole layer of indirection.

A SHELF WITH A SLOT PER COMBINATION OF QUALITIES, USED AS THE GOAL SOURCE. The half that matters — every building reporting what is currently limiting it — needs no grid at all and is in Phase 4. The grid half has a known failure: it fills with a continuum of near-identical blends and the goal stream never stops and never means anything. Gate it on evidence. Run the demand half for three weeks and only build the grid if the world is visibly short of goals. If you do build it, set the bin edges from the Phase 0 closure's actual distribution and then freeze them into the world's version number, because property distributions from a derived chemistry are lopsided and evenly-spaced bins put ninety percent of everything in two cells.

MEASURING "IS THIS AREA PRODUCTIVE" BY COUNTING SOLVES IN THE LAST TEN NIGHTS MINUS THE TEN BEFORE. At ten solves a night spread over thirty clusters, that is a difference of two tiny counts and it is almost entirely noise — and then you feed the noise back as budget, so a cluster that got lucky gets more compute and looks more productive. This does not fail quietly. It manufactures convincing bursts out of nothing, reliably, and you will believe them. A false burst detector is strictly worse than no burst detector when the whole project exists to count real surprises. Use the graded try-count instead, and require a burst to name the building whose version bump is in reach of the solving sites.

E-GRAPHS FOR RECOGNISING THAT TWO DIFFERENTLY-WRITTEN CHUNKS ARE THE SAME TECHNOLOGY. Right tool, wrong year — a second serious systems project bolted onto the first. The 64-bit effect fingerprint gives you the same answer for forty lines: same behaviour on 32 test situations means same technology, one exclusive-or and a bit count.

Also cut: hand-writing the grain cost of each of the 40 commands (derive it from what the command touches); a seventh rule template, ever, for at least six months; hand-placing a good kiln tile; and any goal source whose output you can predict, which is the test to apply to every detector you are ever tempted to add.
