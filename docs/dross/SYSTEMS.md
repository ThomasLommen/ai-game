# The 42 candidate systems, with critic verdicts

Every system proposed for DROSS across seven design lenses, each scored by an adversarial critic on whether it raises the *ceiling* of what kind of surprise is possible or merely the *rate* of familiar ones. Sorted core first.

## Matter & physics


### The Cooling Curve  `core` · 9/10

What a lump is made of does not tell you what it is like. Two lumps with identical ingredients are different substances if one was cooled fast and one slowly. So every lump carries a small extra record alongside its ingredients: which internal arrangement it is in, how coarse its grain is, how much it has been beaten, how full of holes it is. Properties come from the ingredients AND that record. Hardening, tempering, annealing and work-hardening all exist without a single one of them being written down.

**What becomes possible** — Time becomes an ingredient. Two consequences. First, the substance space stops being a smooth landscape you can climb and grows cliffs, because the same recipe on a different schedule lands somewhere completely different — which is the specific cure for the 'you are just hill-climbing a landscape you already drew' failure. Second, and this is why it belongs in Glyphworks specifically: the only way to control a schedule is a building. Bare hands make a thing hot. Only a kiln makes it hot for six hours and then cools it over a day. This is what turns a building from a discount into a capability.

**Example surprise** — Night 118. The compressor names a repeated chunk that runs: heat, hammer, heat, hammer — seven times — then plunge in the river. The output's hardness is off the top of the shelf. It only works because each hammering breaks the grain finer and each reheat re-dissolves what the last one dropped out, and the final fast cool traps a phase that would otherwise vanish. Nobody wrote work-hardening, tempering, or the number seven. The next thing the tribe builds is a shed that holds one temperature so the reheats stop costing a fortune in fuel — and that shed then opens things the hammering was never about.

**Ceiling or rate** — Ceiling, strongly, and it is the cheapest ceiling-raiser of the six. Ingredient space alone is finite and smooth; ingredients-crossed-with-schedules is not, because a schedule is a program and the program space is unbounded. It also raises the rate, because it multiplies the number of distinct outcomes reachable from feedstocks the tribe already holds — the tribe can get somewhere new tonight without finding a new rock.

**How it works** — A substance instance = ingredients int16[20] + a state record:
- phase_frac: float[K], how much of it is in each of the arrangements the draw says this mix can have (usually 1-3)
- grain: float, log scale
- strain: float 0..1, how beaten-up it is
- porosity, moisture: float

While a lump sits in a condition (temperature T, stress):
- It moves toward the arrangement the draw says is stable at T, at a rate given by the standard transformation law: fraction changed X = 1 - exp(-(k*t)^n), with k = A*exp(-E/T). Cool it fast and t is short while k is high, so the change never finishes and the high-temperature arrangement is trapped. That is quenching, for free, and it works differently for every mix because the draw sets which arrangements exist and where.
- Grain grows as d^2 = d0^2 + C*t*exp(-Q/T). Hitting it divides the grain and raises strain. Time above a recovery temperature drops strain back toward zero. That is annealing, for free.

Properties come out as:
- hardness = base(ingredients, arrangement mix) + kHP/sqrt(grain) + kW*strain   (the middle term is the real Hall-Petch law: finer grain, harder metal)
- toughness = base * (1 - brittle-arrangement fraction) * (1 - 0.7*strain)  — so the hard/tough trade-off appears without being written
- permeability from porosity, density from ingredients and porosity

Identity and the stale-answer problem: quantise the state record (grain into 12 log bins, strain into 8, arrangement fractions into sixteenths) and hash it together with the ingredient vector and the core-version from system 1. THAT hash is the substance's identity everywhere else in the program. The search's memory of 'I tried this, here is what happened' is then keyed on something that cannot silently drift, because any change to the chemistry changes the hash and old entries simply stop being found.

**Authored risk** — Medium-low and very specific. You authored the shapes: that fast cooling traps something, that finer grain is harder, that hard trades against tough. You did NOT author which mixes have a trappable arrangement, at what temperature, how fast you must cool to trap it, or whether the trapped thing is any good for anything. So 'there is such a thing as quenching' is yours; 'quenching this one works and quenching that one ruins it' is not. Be honest with yourself: the first time you watch the tribe invent quenching, you are watching your own equation. The fifth time, on a mix you have never looked at, you are watching chemistry you do not know.

**Build cost** — Small to medium. About 400 lines of kinetics plus the quantising and hashing. The AI writes it fast. The real cost is downstream: the substance record grows from roughly 40 bytes to 100, and there are now many more distinct substances, which pressures the search's memory. Budget a week on making the substance store flat arrays with a hash index rather than objects.

**Tuning burden** — Moderate, in month one, then nothing. The rate constants set how many in-world hours a useful heat treatment takes. Get them wrong and either every schedule works (no depth) or none do (dead system). Aim for: a worthwhile treatment takes between one and ten in-world hours, i.e. long enough that a person cannot stand there doing it, which is precisely what forces a building. Expect a week of fiddling.

**Interactions** — Needs The Draw for which arrangements exist and at what temperatures. It is the reason The Walls Decide is worth anything — without path dependence, a building that can hold a steady temperature is only a fuel discount. Feeds The Empty Shelf heavily: most newly-filled property slots will be reached by schedule rather than by recipe. Feeds The Assay, because two lumps with the same ingredients and different histories are exactly the kind of thing a tribe can confuse. Costs throughput.

**Chance it does nothing** — About 20%. The failure mode: the rate constants land somewhere where the tribe's programs cannot stumble into the useful window by accident, so nothing ever notices path dependence exists and it is dead weight carried at some cost. Guard against it by including schedule variation in the day-one reachability audit — vary hold time and cool rate across a grid and count how many extra property slots become reachable. If the answer is 'barely any', fix the constants before you build anything on top.

### The Draw  `core` · 8/10

You never write down what materials exist. When a world is born, the computer rolls about ten numbers for each of roughly twenty made-up elements: how big it is, how greedy it is for other atoms, how tightly it holds onto itself, how heavy, how volatile, how poisonous. Every reaction outcome and every property of every substance is then worked out from those numbers by about eight fixed sums. There is no list of materials in the code and none in your head. A new world is a new roll of the dice, and its metallurgy is genuinely different.

**What becomes possible** — The valley can contain a useful substance neither you nor anyone else has seen. Because the numbers that decide 'these two mix', 'these two form a compound', 'this mixture melts lower than either parent' are drawn rather than written, you cannot look at your own rules and read off what is makeable. It also converts the audit's dreaded monthly chore ('add more physics or it plateaus') into a god-nudge: shift one element's greed number by 10% and the whole downstream tree of what is reachable moves. And it makes a second world worth running, because it is not the same world with different names.

**Example surprise** — Night 210, push notification: 'THE COLD POUR. Element 4 melts at 1420K alone. Element 17 melts at 1610K alone. A 3-to-1 mix melts at 940K — inside the reach of an open fire.' The tribe starts pouring liquid metal before it can fire a clay pot, which is the reverse of the order it happened on Earth, and the whole rest of that valley's history is shaped by it. That dip is a eutectic; you did not put it there, the formula computed it from two drawn numbers.

**Ceiling or rate** — Honestly: on its own, mostly world-to-world variety, plus a modest rate bump inside one world. Its real job is to be the thing the ceiling systems (The Cooling Curve, The Walls Decide) stand on, because a ceiling made of derived numbers can be higher than you know, whereas a ceiling made of twelve hand-written rules is exactly as high as you built it. Build this alone and you get a nicer version of what you have. Build it under systems 3 and 4 and it is what stops the storey count from being something you authored.

**How it works** — Element table: N=20 rows of ~10 floats from a seeded random generator with authored ranges — radius r, pull phi (electronegativity analogue), packing n (electron-density analogue), valence, mass, self-bond energy E0, volatility, oxidation energy, appetite (how much life dislikes it), hue. A substance's ingredients are an int16 count vector over the 20 elements.

Derived by fixed formulas, all real and all one-liners:
- Pair energy H(A,B) = -P*(phiA-phiB)^2 + Q*(nA^(1/3)-nB^(1/3))^2. This is literally Miedema's model, which predicts real alloy formation energies from two numbers per element. Strongly negative = they form a compound; near zero = they dissolve in each other; positive = they refuse to mix.
- Size mismatch delta = sqrt(sum_i c_i*(1 - r_i/rbar)^2). Alloy people use delta<6% plus H in a band to predict a clean solid solution, delta large plus H very negative to predict a glass. Same test here.
- Melting point of a mixture = weighted average minus a dip proportional to |H|*delta. Eutectics fall out.
- Splits and miscibility gaps from the regular-solution rule: a mixture separates below temperature Tc = Omega/2R where Omega comes from H. Free phase diagrams.
- Properties: cohesion from bond energies; hardness from cohesion plus lattice distortion (delta); toughness anti-correlated with hardness through the brittle-phase fraction; conductivity from the packing number; calorific value from oxidation energy; toxicity from appetite.

DAY-ONE GO/NO-GO TOOL (build this before anything else): from the map's raw feedstocks, run forward closure — apply every rule template at a grid of conditions to everything you have, add the products, repeat six times. Count distinct substances and count how many cells of the property shelf (system 5) get filled, and at what depth. A draw that yields fewer than, say, 4 distinct depth-tiers is a dud: reject and redraw. Runs in minutes. This is how you find out in March whether the world has a ceiling, instead of in July.

Throughput note: everything above is arithmetic on short float vectors, so store substances as parallel arrays, not objects, and a reaction evaluation becomes a handful of dot products. That is what lets the nightly search do tens of millions of tries rather than a few million.

**Authored risk** — Be harsh with yourself here. The SHAPE of the formulas is yours, and it guarantees the general facts: some pairs will mix, some will form compounds, eutectics will exist, hard will trade against tough. You will never be surprised that alloys exist. What you will not know is which pairs, at what temperatures, with what properties, and — the thing that actually decides the project — whether the chain of 'this material lets you make that one' bottoms out at storey two or storey six. Compare that honestly against hand-writing fifteen reaction rules, where you know all fifteen outcomes on the day you write them. The bigger hidden authorship is the RANGES you draw from. Pick them badly and every world is the same world with different names; that is the real place your taste re-enters, and no amount of cleverness removes it.

**Build cost** — Small. Roughly 600 lines for the table, the formulas and the substance type, plus 300 for the reachability audit tool. An AI writes it in an afternoon. This is the cheapest system here by a distance.

**Tuning burden** — Real, and concentrated in the draw ranges. Expect two to three weeks of: roll a world, run the reachability audit, look at the numbers, adjust the ranges. You are aiming for something like 'half the rolls produce a chemistry with at least four reachable tiers'. Crucially, you tune by reading the audit output, not by watching the game — which means it is a few evenings of staring at histograms, not months of vibes. After that it is near zero, and rerolling becomes fun rather than a chore.

**Interactions** — Everything needs this. Systems 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 all read numbers off the element table. It fights with nothing directly, but it makes the naming model's job harder (there is no 'iron' to recognise), which is exactly why system 6 exists. It also makes the memory-of-past-attempts problem easier if you do one thing: hash the element table itself into a core-version number and put that number inside every substance identity, so nothing computed under one chemistry can ever be reused under another.

**Chance it does nothing** — About 25% on its own terms, and I would say closer to 50% if you build it without system 3. The failure mode is precise and one of the audits already named it: derived properties may turn out to be a smooth landscape over the ingredient mix, so the reachable set is one connected blob and 'discovery' becomes hill-climbing a hill you can see the shape of from the start. The defences are thresholds (melting, phase splits, which chop the blob) and path dependence (system 3, which adds an axis that is not made of ingredients at all).

### The Ledger  `core` · 8/10

Atoms are counted. Every reaction moves whole atoms from one pile to another, and nothing is created or destroyed; the same goes for heat. So everything the tribe did not want still exists and has to be somewhere: on the ground as slag, dissolved in the river, or up in the air. The valley's air and water are just two more piles with a mix in them, and they change as the tribe works. That one rule makes waste into a real substance with a real location, and makes the tribe's own success able to poison it.

**What becomes possible** — Two things that cannot happen without it. First, by-products: materials that only exist because some other process threw them away, which is the natural source of the second-tier stuff the whole design is betting on. Second, a problem generator that never runs out and that scales with how good the tribe gets — as output climbs, concentrations climb, and the world starts breaking in places nobody was working. That is the direct answer to 'the world runs out of new problems in 6-10 weeks': this one gets harder as the tribe gets better.

**Example surprise** — Night 318: 'RIVER: element 12 is up six-fold since night 240. Source is the roast at site 148. Fish yield at sites 150-166 has halved.' Programs that worked for a year start failing downstream of the tribe's own furnaces, and nobody wrote pollution — element 12 just happened to be drawn volatile and drawn with a strongly negative appetite number, and mass balance did the rest.

The other flavour is the one he actually cares about: the slag heap outside the furnace is, by mass balance, concentrated in the one element that refused to alloy. That pile turns out to melt at 1900K, higher than any rock in the valley. It becomes the lining of the next furnace. The waste of tier one is the wall of tier two — craft becoming industry — and it arrived by arithmetic, not by design.

**Ceiling or rate** — Both, and it is the only one here that clearly does both. Ceiling: by-products are an entire extra source of feedstock that does not exist until technology exists, so the material tree can climb through stuff that is not in the ground anywhere. Rate: pollution and depletion generate fresh, located, unscripted problems forever, and their supply is tied to the tribe's own output, so the supply cannot dry up while the tribe is still improving.

**How it works** — - Every stockpile, tile deposit, atmosphere cell and river cell is an int32 array of element counts plus fixed-point joules. Nothing anywhere is a named item.
- A reaction is an integer transfer between count vectors plus an energy transfer. In debug builds, assert sum-before == sum-after on both. That single assert is the most valuable bug net in the project, and it catches exactly the class of silent wrong answer the audit is scared of, because a stale or mismatched result almost always fails to balance.
- Volatiles: each element has a boil point derived from the draw; above it, counts move from the lump to the tile's atmosphere cell. The atmosphere is a coarse grid (a few hundred cells over the valley) with a wind direction and slow mixing.
- Water: river cells carry dissolved counts downstream; how much dissolves comes from the pull number.
- Life coupling, one line: growth and health get multiplied by product over elements of (1 - appetite_e * concentration_e), with appetite drawn in system 1. So which element is the poison is not your choice.
- Deposits are finite counts. When a tile runs out, it runs out, and the economic centre of the map moves on its own.
- Works decay becomes chemistry rather than a tuned number: a kiln lining reacts with what it holds using the same pair-energy formula, so upkeep is literally replacing consumed lining, and a kiln used for the wrong thing eats itself fast.

**Authored risk** — Low. Conservation is a referee, not content — it removes possibilities rather than adding authored ones. The one seam is the life-coupling line: you decide that composition harms life and roughly how hard. Keep it to the single product formula above with the appetite coefficients drawn, and you have not chosen the poison, only the existence of poison. Resist any urge to hand-place a 'safe' element.

**Build cost** — Medium, and invasive rather than large — about 800 lines, but it touches every data structure in the simulation. Put it in on day one. Retrofitting conservation into a running world is a fortnight of misery and the AI writing the code does not save you from that, because the bugs will be in the seams between old and new representations.

**Tuning burden** — Moderate and mostly in the movement rates: how fast air mixes, how fast the river carries things away. Get it wrong and pollution either never bites or wipes the valley out by night 60. Expect a few rounds. Depletion is easier because the map's element counts are large. One good self-check: plot concentration of each element in air and water over 200 nights; you want slow drift with occasional local spikes, not flat lines and not exponentials.

**Interactions** — Needs The Draw for volatility and appetite. Feeds The Empty Shelf (by-product piles fill property slots nobody aimed at, which is where the best surprises come from). Makes works maintenance honest instead of a hand-tuned decay number. Costs some throughput — every reaction is a few more array operations. Slight tension with the compressor: a short program can be a filthy one, and nothing in code-shortening notices that it dumps element 12 in the river.

**Chance it does nothing** — About 10% that conservation is wasted — it is not, it earns its keep as a bug net alone. But about 30% that the pollution loop specifically fails to be interesting, and the likeliest reason is scale: if the tribe's output is tiny relative to the valley's air volume, nothing ever concentrates and the mechanism sits idle for years. Make the atmosphere cells small and the valley enclosed. It is a valley for a reason.

### The Walls Decide  `core` · 7/10

Do not write twelve physics rules. Write six rule SHAPES with blanks in them: 'if this quantity goes past THIS NUMBER while that one sits between THESE, the stuff turns into that'; 'hold this condition for THIS LONG and the state drifts toward there at THIS RATE'. Bare hands can only fill those blanks with feeble numbers — a brief flare of heat, no containment, no pressure, no steady anything. A building fills the blanks with the numbers its own walls can physically survive, worked out from the properties of the material it happens to be made of. So a building is not a discount, it is a licence to use a part of the rulebook that nobody in the valley could reach before. How many tiers of building are possible is then decided by the chemistry, not by you.

**What becomes possible** — The rulebook stops being a fixed length. New physical behaviour becomes available not because you added a rule but because somebody made a better wall. That is the difference between reaching further along a ladder you built and growing the ladder. It also deletes both of the authored seams the audit found: the hand-written table mapping program syntax to build costs disappears (build cost is now 'the materials needed to survive the conditions you want to hold', which is arithmetic), and the hand-tuned classifier that decides which chunks become buildings disappears (a chunk becomes buildable when it repeatedly asks for conditions bare hands cannot hold, which is measured off the execution trace).

**Example surprise** — Night 402, push: 'NEW REGIME — site 148 held 1780K for four hours. That is 260K past anything ever sustained in this valley. The lining is the grey powder left over from the salt-roast at site 91. Three threshold rules that have never once fired in 402 nights are now live at that tile.' And what then fires is a reduction nobody has ever seen, because the temperature it needed was drawn at 1740K and was, until tonight, simply outside the world. You did not know that rule existed either — it is one of thousands of latent instances the draw generated and nothing had ever been able to trip.

**Ceiling or rate** — This is THE ceiling system, and it is the only one of the six that raises the ceiling rather than the reach. Everything else grows what is reachable under a fixed rulebook; this makes the set of ACTIVE rules a function of what has been built. ### The Empty Shelf  `strong` · 7/10

Keep a shelf with a slot for every rough combination of qualities: hard-and-light-and-heatproof, soft-and-sticky-and-burns-well, and so on. Six or eight qualities, eight steps each. Whenever the tribe makes something, it goes in the matching slot along with the cheapest known way to make it. Every night the world hands out goals of the form 'make something that would sit in this empty slot next door to a full one'. The goals therefore grow out of what has already been achieved and can never be finished, because filling a slot creates fresh empty neighbours. The important second half: only chase empty slots whose quality would fix something that is currently breaking.

**What becomes possible** — The world poses problems you never wrote and could not have written, because the problems are stated in terms of substances that do not exist yet. 'Something tougher than the thing that keeps snapping' is a sentence you cannot put in a rules file, but the world can compute it every night from what actually failed. This is the direct answer to the audit's finding that the supply of problems, not the compressor, is the bottleneck everywhere.

**Example surprise** — Night 274: 'The valley has wanted something lighter than water and stiff for 60 nights. Tonight it got one: bubbles. A melt at site 90 was stirred with a plant that gives off gas when it burns, and the froth set hard.' Foam is in no rule you wrote — it is a gas-evolution rule (template 6) firing inside a melt that happens to be viscous, with the porosity field from system 3 doing the rest, aimed at by a demand for low density that came from a raft that kept sinking.

**Ceiling or rate** — Rate, primarily, and it raises it a lot. Ceiling only indirectly. I want to be straight about this: filling in a shelf is reach, not height. Its real value is that it keeps the tribe pressing against the edge of what it can do, night after night, for long enough that the rare ceiling events — a new regime, a new wall material, a by-product that turns out to be better than the product — actually get a chance to happen. Without it, the world goes quiet in week eight and the ceiling systems never get their shot. With it, the world is still trying things in month nine.

**How it works** — - Grid over 6-8 derived property axes, 6-10 bins each. Store sparse: hash map from packed bin index to a record.

- Nightly: take every filled slot, generate its empty neighbours (one bin step on one axis). That is the frontier — typically a few thousand slots.
- DEMAND SCORE, which is the half that matters: every standing works and every tool in use reports its LIMITING PROPERTY — the smallest margin between what it has and what it needs (the wall that is closest to melting, the rope closest to snapping, the process closest to running out of fuel value). Take the top ~50 by demand as tonight's goals, plus ~10 drawn at random from the frontier so the world does not only chase its own nose.
- Retire a goal once it can be solved cheaply, so the goal list stays in the band between trivial and impossible.
- Slots the day-one reachability audit proved unreachable under this draw stay legal goals and are never filled. Real material property charts have permanent holes for exactly this reason; so should yours, and it is honest not to hide them.

**Authored risk** — Low to moderate, with one genuine seam. You choose the axes, and properties you do not measure cannot be aimed at — so keep the axis list to quantities that already exist for physical reasons (density, hardness, toughness, melting point, conductivity, calorific value, permeability, toxicity) rather than to things that sound like technologies ('sharpness', 'usefulness as a container'). The demand scoring is where your taste could sneak in: if you hand-weight what the civilization wants, you are back to authoring the tech tree with extra steps. Derive demand from actual recorded failures and nothing else.

**Build cost** — Small. About 400 lines, half a day for an AI. The demand scoring needs the works layer to report its limiting property, which is another 100 lines inside system 4.

**Tuning burden** — Light but ongoing: how many goals per night, how wide the bins, how much random exploration. The failure signal is easy to see — if the map fills with near-identical substances, the bins are too fine. One number to watch weekly: the fraction of newly-filled slots that any program ever uses afterwards.

**Interactions** — Needs derived properties (system 1) and really needs path dependence (system 3), or it will just blend ingredients and interpolate. Feeds the nightly search directly with goals. Feeds the phone view beautifully: the shelf drawn as a grid with lit cells, next to the map, is a four-second check-in and the time-lapse of it filling over six months is as good an artefact as the map itself. ### The Assay  `later` · 5/10

The tribe cannot see what things are made of or what their numbers are. A lump has real hidden properties, and the only way a program learns anything is by doing something to it and watching — hit it and see if it shatters, drop it in water and see if it floats, put it in a fire and count how long before it changes. What the tribe knows about a substance is a set of measurements, and two lumps are the same thing to them only if no measurement has ever told them apart. A name is earned at the moment a measurement first separates two things.

**What becomes possible** — Matter can be misunderstood. A tribe can believe two different substances are one substance, use them interchangeably for two hundred nights, and be wrong — until a building made of the wrong one fails. That whole category of event is impossible when everything carries a label. It also makes MEASUREMENT a technology with tiers of its own, because some tests can only be done inside a building that holds a steady condition, so better buildings mean finer distinctions mean the tribe can aim at things it previously could not even see.

**Example surprise** — Night 356: 'THE SPLIT. What the tribe has called grey-heavy for 200 nights is two different things. The kiln at site 148 failed. A new test — time to soften over a held fire, only possible since the kiln existed — separates them cleanly. The good one comes from the north bank only.' An entire reorganisation of where the tribe digs and builds follows, triggered by a measurement that only became possible because of a building, which was only built because of an earlier misunderstanding.

**Ceiling or rate** — Mostly rate, some ceiling. Rate: being wrong is a renewable source of trouble, and trouble is problems, and problems are what the world is short of. Ceiling: it adds a second ladder running parallel to the material one — the tribe's ability to tell things apart — and the two feed each other, because finer distinctions let the search aim at targets it could not previously name, and better buildings enable finer distinctions. Separately, and this matters for whether he can trust his own world: it fixes the fake-depth problem. The small language model is only asked to name things the tribe has actually told apart by an experiment, and it is given the DISTINGUISHING measurement to name rather than a lump of code. So every name in the world corresponds to a real difference somebody measured.

**How it works** — - The true substance is the record from systems 1 and 3. A tribe holds a KIND TABLE: kind_id -> the set of (test_id, observed value bucket) pairs ever recorded for lumps filed under that kind, plus the list of true substance hashes that have been filed there.
- A TEST is just a short program whose result is a number: strike-and-count-pieces, float-in-water, hold-in-fire-and-time-to-change, grind-and-weigh, cool-and-see-if-it-cracks. Tests are found by the same search that finds everything else. Some need conditions only a works can hold, which is what gives tests tiers.
- Filing: when a lump is picked up, the habitual cheap tests run (this costs execution steps, which is why the tribe will not measure everything); the resulting measurement vector is matched to the nearest kind within a tolerance. No match, new kind.
- SPLIT: when a test produces a two-humped spread of results within one existing kind, split the kind. One line of statistics, and a superb push notification.
- MERGE: two kinds that no test has ever separated get merged.
- Naming: the model is invited only when a kind is minted or split, and it is handed the distinguishing measurement ('softens slower than the other under a held fire, and sinks') rather than a repeated code pattern. Naming a difference is a job a small model can actually do; naming a repeated fragment of code is a job it only pretends to do.
- Programs refer to kinds, never to true substances. So a program can be silently wrong about matter — and unlike a silent wrong answer in the search, this kind of wrongness is a feature, because it produces a visible failure in the world with a readable cause.

**Authored risk** — Very low, and it is the cleanest of the six on this axis. You author the requirement that things must be measured to be known. You author no measurement, no kind, and no name. The tests are discovered by the same search as everything else.

**Build cost** — Medium. About 700 lines, plus a layer of indirection between programs and matter that the search has to go through, which is the fiddly part. Roughly a week and a half of AI-written code and rather more of your debugging, because bugs here look like the tribe behaving stupidly rather than like crashes.

**Tuning burden** — Mostly the matching tolerance: too loose and everything is one grey kind, too tight and the world has ten thousand kinds and no names mean anything. There is a good self-tuning rule available — set the tolerance from the natural spread of the test's own results, so a noisy test separates less than a precise one, automatically. Do that and the burden mostly disappears.

**Interactions** — Needs systems 1 and 3 to have anything hidden worth finding — with hand-authored materials there is nothing to be wrong about. Gets much better with system 4, because works-tier tests are what make late splits possible. Costs throughput, since programs now spend steps measuring. Mild tension with The Empty Shelf, which is written in true properties: keep the shelf as the world's own bookkeeping and let the tribe aim only through its kinds, and accept that the tribe will sometimes aim at the wrong thing, which is the point.

**Chance it does nothing** — About 40%, joint highest, and for a different reason from the others: it may be pure friction. The interesting middle might be narrow or might not exist. **How it works** — Table `reached(vec[8] quantized, first_night, tile)` kept as an unstructured list rather than a fixed grid — a new point is only added if its distance to every existing point exceeds a threshold, which means new axes can be introduced later without rebuilding anything. Nightly: sample up to 40 candidate goals by (a) take a reached point, multiply one axis by 1.15-1.4; (b) take two reached points that differ on different axes, demand the union; (c) take a reached point, triple its duration requirement; (d) demand a material whose property record lands in a region no material has ever landed in. Survival predicates stay exactly as they are — they remain the lethal floor. These are the ceiling. Roughly 200 lines plus the screening runs, which cost maybe 5-10% of a night's compute.

The saving grace is that the screen is measured, not judged — it asks the search, not you.

**Build cost** — Two to three evenings once the hold vector exists. The screening runs need the search to be re-entrant with a budget parameter, which it should be anyway. Cheapest ceiling-per-line in this whole document.

**Tuning burden** — Light after the first fortnight. Feeds System 2: every regime lost to collapse instantly re-enters the goal list, so the ruin cycle is also a problem generator.

**Chance it does nothing** — Maybe 20%. ### The Hold  `core` · 8/10

Right now a building is a shortcut: it makes an expensive chunk of code cheap at one spot. Change it so a building's real job is to PIN NUMBERS. Every building stores a short list of numbers it keeps steady at its tile — how hot, how much air moving through, how sealed, how much weight it can brace, how long it can keep that going. Call that list its hold. The physics is then rewritten as six formulas that all read the local hold, so at a tile with a hot, sealed hold, materials behave in ways they simply cannot behave anywhere else in the valley. And the numbers a building can hold are not chosen by you or by the naming model: they are computed from the stuff it was built out of, so a building's power is capped by the worst material in its walls.

**What becomes possible** — Two things the current design cannot do. First, the number of technology rungs stops being a thing you wrote down. Today, each new 'regime' is one rule-guard you relaxed by hand, so twelve rules gives three to five rungs and then it is sprawl. With hold-driven formulas, a rung exists wherever a new held number crosses a threshold that lives in a material's own property record — so how many rungs exist is arithmetic over the materials, not a list. Second, and this is the one he actually cares about: it makes second-order buildings mechanically necessary rather than lucky. To hold a bigger number you need a better wall. A better wall is a material. If the only materials that survive that heat are ones that have to be COOKED at that heat, then the only way up is a building made from a thing another building made. That is not a special case bolted on; it is the default path.

**Example surprise** — Night 214, notification reads: WALL FROM WASTE — a works called 'greyjaw' built at tile 188, walls made of the grey crust that the smelting works at tile 191 has been throwing away for sixty nights. Holds 1,340 where the valley record was 980. Nobody ever wrote that the by-product was a building material; the by-product just happened to have a melt point higher than anything raw in the valley, and the search found it because the goal generator kept asking for a hotter hold and the only material in reach that survived the test was the slag heap next door.

**Ceiling or rate** — Ceiling, directly and mostly. It converts 'how many kinds of thing can ever happen here' from a number you authored (one per rule guard) into a number that falls out of how far composite materials can overshoot their parents. Honest arithmetic: if each generation of composite can beat its best parent by 8-15% on the heat-tolerance axis with diminishing returns, and each new rung needs roughly 1.3x the previous held value, you get somewhere around eight to fourteen rungs instead of three to five. That is not infinite and I will not pretend it is. But it roughly triples the storey count AND makes each storey something you find out about rather than something you scheduled.

**How it works** — Data. Every material is a sparse record of ~8 numbers (hardness, toughness, melt point, ignite point, wetness, density, grain, reactivity). Table `works(id, tile, chunk_id, built_night, wall_mat_id, wall_qty, hold[8] floats, condition 0..1, footprint)`. Table `tile_hold(tile, hold[8], stamp)` recomputed whenever works change; hold at a tile is the per-axis maximum over works whose footprint covers it or sits within radius r. Physics is six formulas, not twelve rules: (1) CHANGE — property B of a material drifts toward a target at a rate computed from (local held value minus that material's own threshold) / density; (2) JOIN — two materials put together under a held value produce a composite whose record is a weighted blend PLUS an overshoot term on one axis; (3) SPLIT — above a held value a material separates into a high-B part and a low-B part in a ratio set by its grain; (4) MOVE — a held motion or airflow value shifts stuff between tiles at a rate; (5) KEEP — under a held value a property stops drifting back toward ambient; (6) COUPLE — two adjacent holds combine into a third (hot next to airflow gives effective heat = heat * (1 + 0.6*airflow)). Every blank in those formulas is filled from either a material's own record or the local hold. Nothing is filled by a table you wrote. Build cost: to hold value v on axis j you need wall material whose tolerance on j is at least v, in quantity proportional to v and to footprint. That single line DELETES the hand-written syntax-to-build-cost table the audit called a recipe book in disguise — a kiln costs what it costs because of what it has to survive. Stress = held value / wall tolerance, per axis, which System 2 uses. When you tap a building on your phone it shows the extra lines of physics active at that tile and nowhere else, which is the readable version of 'the rulebook grew'.

**Authored risk** — High and I want to be blunt about where. The six formulas are 100% yours. The eight property axes are 100% yours and choosing 'melt point' as an axis is choosing that fire matters. The single most authored number in the entire project is the overshoot term in the JOIN formula, because it alone decides whether a composite can ever beat its parents on the axis that gates wall quality. Set it to zero — make composites a pure blend of their parents, which is the natural, tidy, physically-plausible-looking choice — and the probability of a second-order building is exactly zero, forever, and you will never get an error message telling you so. That is the most dangerous sentence in this document. What is NOT authored: which held values get reached, in what order, at which tiles, out of which materials, and therefore which rungs the valley actually has. The list is gone; the arithmetic remains yours.

**Build cost** — The formula rewrite plus the works and tile_hold tables plus the hold-aware evaluator: about a week of evenings for the code. The tail is longer — expect two to four weeks of watching runs where nothing gets built, or everything does, while you find out that your material tolerance numbers are half an order of magnitude off. This replaces existing Glyphworks work rather than adding to it, so it is not net-new on top; it is the same month spent on a better shape.

**Tuning burden** — Real and unavoidable, but it is honest tuning rather than taste-tuning. You will be moving: the overshoot term (once, carefully, with a script that answers 'is there any chain of joins that reaches 1500 degrees?' before you ever run the sim); the wall-quantity-per-held-value coefficient, which sets whether buildings are affordable at all; and the eight tolerance numbers per material. Write a five-minute offline checker that, given the material table and the formulas, brute-forces four levels of joins and prints the best achievable value on each axis at each level. If that printout is flat after level two, you have no second-order buildings and no amount of simulation will produce them. Run it before every run. That checker is worth more than a month of watching.

**Interactions** — Everything hangs off this. System 3 generates goals in hold-space, so it needs the hold vector to exist. System 4's compound buildings combine hold vectors. System 5 caps hold by terrain. System 2's decay is driven by stress, which is only definable once a wall has a tolerance. If you build one thing from this document, build this, because the other five are cheap once it exists and worthless without it.

**Chance it does nothing** — Maybe 25% that it delivers roughly what the current design already would. The failure mode is not a crash: it is that your material table has a short range, the offline checker shows four rungs, and you have done a week of work for the same three-to-five storeys with a better story about why. The checker is what makes this knowable in an afternoon instead of a month. System 2 and System 5 are the defences and both are needed.

### The Counting House  `core` · 5/10

Every building quietly remembers the programs that worked at it — the last few hundred successful ones, stored on the building. Tomorrow's search at that tile starts from those instead of from nothing, and a building also grants its tile extra thinking budget. So a place that has been worked at for a long time is a place where the search goes deeper for the same cost, which means the strangest inventions cluster around old buildings. The other half of this system is the boring half that makes the rest safe: the search remembers 'I tried this program, here is what happened', and what a program does depends on which buildings are in reach, so every remembered answer is stamped with a fingerprint of the local buildings and thrown away when that fingerprint changes.

**What becomes possible** — A place can become special for a computational reason and can then be lost. That is a new kind of object in the world: not a thing that produces a material, but a thing that makes the tribe cleverer nearby, which can burn down. It also solves the practical wall — blind brute force stalls past about three layers deep, and seeding from the local memory is the cheapest possible guidance because the seeds are real programs that really worked in really this place. And the stamped memory is what stops the whole map going quietly wrong six weeks in, which is the failure mode that would waste a month of his life with no error message.

**Example surprise** — Night 291: a small, ugly works at tile 55 that produces nothing anyone can name has accumulated 400 remembered programs over 90 nights, because it happens to sit where three regions of terrain meet and everyone passes through. Solve rates within two tiles of it are running at four times the valley average. On night 313 a fire takes it. Over the following month the notification stream from that end of the valley goes almost silent — new chunks accepted per night drops from about three to under one — and the tribe's next two inventions both happen at the other end of the map. Then someone reads the ruin, recovers about half the memory, and rebuilds. That whole arc is a story about a library burning down, and there is no word for library anywhere in the code.

**Ceiling or rate** — Rate, mostly, and a modest ceiling effect. Deeper reachable search means deeper composites, and depth is what the rare events are made of, so it does push the ceiling a little. But be honest: seeding search with past successes narrows as much as it deepens, and a place with a strong memory will tend to keep doing variations of what it already does. The stamped-memory half raises nothing at all — its entire value is that it stops the ceiling from being fake.

**How it works** — Memory: `works_memory(works_id, program_id, last_used_night, times_used)`, capped at K (start at 256) with least-recently-used eviction. At the start of a search at a tile, load the memory of every works in reach and inject those programs into the search's starting set as if they were already-built parts, at zero cost. Also add a flat node-budget bonus per works in reach. Stamping: tile_stamp = a 64-bit hash of the sorted list of (works_id, hold vector quantized to about 16 steps per axis, condition bucketed into 5 levels). Every remembered search result is keyed by (what the program did on the sample states, tile_stamp). Any build, any collapse, any decay that crosses a condition bucket bumps the stamp for every tile in radius r; old-stamp entries are not hunted down and deleted, they are simply never matched again and fall out by least-recently-used. Quantizing the hold and bucketing the condition is what stops routine nightly wear from churning the whole memory every night. Tripwire, and this is the part he must not skip: one in every 500 memory hits gets re-executed for real and compared to the remembered answer. On mismatch, log loudly, dump the stamp, the program, both answers, and halt the night. Without that tripwire, a subtle stamping bug shows up as a map that has quietly been wrong for three weeks.

**Authored risk** — Very low on the memory side — you author K and the budget bonus, nothing about content. Zero on the stamping side, which is pure plumbing. The one soft risk: seeding from local memory is a thumb on the scale that favours doing what was done here before, which will make places look more specialised than the physics alone would justify. That is arguably good (it is how real crafts work) but do not then claim the specialisation was discovered from nothing; it was partly manufactured by the seeding.

**Build cost** — Memory and seeding: two evenings. Stamping done properly: about a week, and it is the fiddliest week in the project, exactly as the audit says. The tripwire is one afternoon and pays for itself the first time it fires. Budget a further week for the first real stamping bug, because there will be one and it will not look like a stamping bug — it will look like the physics being wrong.

**Tuning burden** — K and the quantisation step. Too fine a quantisation and the memory churns constantly and you lose the speed-up entirely; too coarse and you get answers computed for a slightly different set of buildings, which the tripwire will catch but which will also cost you a debugging evening. Start coarse enough to be fast, keep the tripwire on permanently, and let it tell you when you have gone too far. That is the whole methodology and it removes most of the judgement.

**Interactions** — Required by Systems 1, 4 and 5, all of which make the local building set change what programs do — without stamping, all three of them are actively dangerous. Feeds System 2, since a building's memory dies with it and is partially recoverable from its ruin, which is what makes the library-burning story possible. Slightly fights System 3: a strong local memory makes the search conservative, and the goal generator's job is to keep dragging it away from what it already knows.

**Chance it does nothing** — The memory half: about 35% that it just makes the search faster at doing the same things, with no visible clustering of invention and no story. The stamping half: 0% chance it does nothing, because it is not optional — either you build it or the project produces wrong answers that look like results. If you are ranking these six by 'must exist', this is second after System 1, and its boring half is the reason.

### Stress, Upkeep and the Ruin Layer  `strong` · 7/10

Buildings that push hardest break soonest. A building's wear per night is proportional to the square of how close its held numbers are to what its walls can actually take — so the record-setting kiln is always the most fragile thing in the valley. As it wears, it quietly holds LESS, so its output gets worse before it dies. When it collapses it leaves a ruin: a row storing its held numbers with noise on them, and its stored program with random pieces punched out. A later tribe can read that ruin, which hands the search a program with holes in it instead of a blank page — so rediscovering something is genuinely cheaper than discovering it. And the rubble itself still holds a bit of what the building held, and counts as part of a new wall, so tribes build on their own wreckage.

**What becomes possible** — Three things. (1) Losing a rung. Right now nothing in Glyphworks can take a capability AWAY in a way the tribe can feel and respond to; floods destroy buildings but that is just damage. Stress-decay means the top of the ladder is inherently unstable, so the valley oscillates around its own frontier rather than ratcheting flat, and every oscillation is another few thousand attempts at the hard problem. (2) A real ratchet across dead civilizations: a tribe seeded a thousand nights later does not start from zero, it starts from holey programs and half-standing walls, so the long-run trend goes up even though every individual tribe fails. (3) Over-building as an actual way to die: upkeep is material per night, so a valley that built forty works starves in a bad winter and has to choose which ones to let fall — you get to watch a civilization abandon its periphery, and which ones it keeps is a decision nobody authored.

**Example surprise** — Night 402: a tribe reads the drowned kiln at tile 74, which has been rubble for 180 nights. One hole in the recovered program sat where a variable used to be; the search filled it with a constant, because a constant is shorter and the search tries short things first. The rebuilt kiln runs that step at a fixed value slightly above anything the original ever used, and holds 6% hotter than the building it was copied from. The notification says: REBUILT WRONG, RUNS BETTER. The map shows the new works standing on the old one's footprint, and the family tree of chunks now has a visible fork with a note that one parent is a guess.

**Ceiling or rate** — Mostly rate, and I will not oversell it. It manufactures repeat attempts at the hardest currently-open problem, which is the single biggest driver of how often a big thing fires. But there is one genuine ceiling effect and it is the ruin ratchet: because a later tribe starts from partial programs, the achievable depth over a year is not capped by what one tribe can reach in one lifetime. Without ruins, six months is six months of one civilization's luck. With ruins, it is a relay.

**How it works** — Per night, per works: stress = max over axes of (held value / wall tolerance on that axis). condition -= base_decay * stress^2 * (1 + local weather term). Upkeep = wall_qty * base_decay * stress^2, drawn as actual material from the tribe's stock; unpaid upkeep doubles the decay. Below condition 0.6, held values scale by condition — the kiln literally stops being able to hold what it held, so its products drift out of the range the formulas need, and programs that used to work start failing. That failure is the interesting one because there is no error, only worse pots. At condition 0 the row moves to `ruin(id, tile, hold[8] with ±20% noise, holey_body blob, legibility 0..1, buried_depth, died_night)`. legibility = condition_at_death * wall material durability * exp(-nights_since/tau). holey_body: walk the stored program tree, blank each subtree independently with probability (1 - legibility). A `read-ruin` command returns that holey program; the search then only has to fill the holes, which collapses the space it has to look through by orders of magnitude — that is the whole mechanism of archaeology and it is about forty lines. Rubble terrain: the ruin's tile keeps 25% of the ruin's hold on axes where the wall material is stone-like (nothing for wattle), and rebuilding on a ruin tile counts existing rubble as 40% of required wall_qty. Also fold the naming gate in here: the small language model names NOTHING until a chunk has either been built as a works or been called twenty times. **Build cost** — Small. A weekend for decay, upkeep, collapse and the ruin table. Another weekend for read-ruin and the hole-filling search seed, plus the map rendering of ruins in grey. The debugging tail is mild because failures here are loud (buildings vanish) rather than silent.

Too much decay and nothing ever stands long enough to be built on top of, which kills second-order buildings dead. Too little and the valley ossifies and you are back to sprawl. ### The Yard  `strong` · 7/10

Buildings can be used together. A program running at a tile can call any building within a short radius, and the numbers held at that tile are the best of what all those buildings hold — plus, for a handful of pairs, a coupling: air moving next to heat gives you an effective heat higher than either alone. Then the compressor does its usual job on the programs that worked, and when a repeated chunk turns out to call two or more nearby buildings, that chunk itself becomes buildable as a single building whose held numbers are the combined set. Do that twice and you have a building made of buildings made of buildings, and nowhere in the code is there a word for workshop, village or factory.

**What becomes possible** — A second route to the ceiling that does not require a better wall. Right now the only way to hold a bigger number is better material. Coupling means two ordinary buildings arranged correctly reach a value neither can reach alone — so a tribe with poor materials can still climb, by arranging. That roughly doubles the number of paths to any given rung, and paths are what determine whether a rare event ever fires. It also creates a genuinely new kind of object: a building whose only purpose is to sit between two others. Nothing in the current design can produce a component that has no output.

**Example surprise** — Night 268: three works in a line at tiles 140, 141, 142. The middle one produces nothing, holds nothing anyone asked for, and is called by no program directly. It exists because the coupling formula gives 141 an airflow value that raises the effective heat at both 140 and 142 above what either could reach alone, and the search kept choosing layouts that had it. Two weeks later the compressor pulls out a chunk that calls all three and the tribe builds the whole line as one works, named by the model as 'longbreath', with a single hold vector. Your map now shows one building where three stood, and the family tree shows it has three parents that are themselves buildings. If a second-order building is the moment craft becomes industry, this is the moment a workshop becomes a machine.

**Ceiling or rate** — Ceiling. Coupling multiplies rather than adds: an airflow of 0.8 next to heat 900 gives effective 1,332, which may be a rung the material table cannot otherwise reach at that point in history. And compound buildings recurse — a compound can be a member of another compound — so the depth of stacking is limited by upkeep economics rather than by any number you wrote. This is the single biggest lever on the probability of the event he cares about, because it means the second-order building can arrive by arrangement even in a run where the material chemistry got unlucky.

**How it works** — Radius r (start at 1 tile, i.e. the six neighbours on a hex) defines reach. tile_hold = per-axis max over works in reach, then apply coupling passes: a small set of 3-4 coupling formulas (heat x airflow, water head x containment, heat x containment giving pressure, motion x grain giving grind rate), each applied once, in fixed order, to fixed point. Compound promotion, nightly, after the compressor: for each accepted chunk, check whether its body calls two or more works that all lie within one radius of a common tile, and whether its run cost is above a threshold. If yes it enters the buildable list as a compound whose hold vector is the coupled combination, whose wall requirement is the sum of members' walls at a 15% discount, and whose per-call cost is a further fraction. Members are consumed into it — the rows are marked as parts, they keep decaying, and if a part dies the compound's hold drops to whatever the survivors give. Footprints matter: each works occupies tiles and you cannot overlap, so radius r holds a bounded number of buildings and the tribe must choose a layout. That constraint alone is what makes clusters look like settlements rather than piles.

**Authored risk** — Medium-high and I will name it precisely: the 3-4 coupling formulas are yours, and 'air makes fire hotter' is you putting the bellows in the box before the game starts. That is real authored content and you should count it as such. The defence is that you author the COUPLING and not the OBJECT — you never say what the air-moving thing is made of, where it stands, what shape its program has, or that anyone should want one. In practice, expect an observer to say the bellows was designed and the three-building line was not, and they will be right about both.

**Build cost** — Radius reach and the max-per-axis rollup: an evening. Coupling passes: an evening. Compound promotion including the part-death semantics: about a week of evenings, because 'a part of a compound decays' is where the bugs live. Rendering compounds as one glyph with a part count is another evening and is worth it, because a map where three buildings merge into one in the time-lapse is the best single frame this project can produce.

**Tuning burden** — Radius r is the whole game and you will change it several times. r=1 gives tight clusters and heavy competition for tiles; r=2 gives sprawling towns and much weaker geography. The compound promotion threshold is a second dial and it is exactly the kind of taste-seam the audit warned about — hold it honest by making the threshold economic (a chunk gets promoted if the upkeep of the compound is less than the total upkeep of the parts plus the run-cost saving over the last 30 nights) rather than a hand-set number.

**Interactions** — Sits directly on System 1. Constrained by System 2, which decides how big a cluster can be maintained. Constrained by System 5, which decides where couplings are even possible. Makes System 6's stale-answer problem much worse, because a compound changes the held numbers of every tile in reach at once — the version stamp has to cover compounds properly or you get exactly the silent wrong answers the audit warned about.

**Chance it does nothing** — About 30% that compounds never fire, and the reason is mundane: the promotion test requires a chunk that calls two nearby buildings AND is expensive AND recurs often enough for the compressor to notice. If radius is small and buildings are scarce in the first months, that conjunction may just not happen before you get bored. Mitigation: log every night how many accepted chunks call two-or-more works, even the ones that fail the cost test. If that number is zero for three weeks, coupling is not being used and the problem is upstream, in the goal generator, not here.

### Ground  `cheap-win` · 8/10

Terrain holds numbers for free, and terrain also limits what a building can hold there. A gorge holds moving air. A cave holds a steady temperature and darkness. Rapids hold motion. A rock terrace lets you brace against something, so you can hold weight and pressure; silt does not, and a building that tries to hold pressure on silt blows out. So a building's ceiling on each axis is the smaller of what its walls can take and what the ground will support. The first buildings in the valley are therefore found rather than built, and the high rungs of the ladder only exist in certain places on the map.

**What becomes possible** — Geography becomes a variable in invention rather than scenery. Two tribes with the same library and the same materials end up with different technologies because one of them lives near a gorge. It gives the map a reason to have industrial districts that nobody placed, it gives migration a computed motive, and it means a flood that changes the river also changes which rungs of the ladder exist where — a physical event that removes an entire branch of possible technology rather than just breaking some huts. It also gives the early game a free ladder: a cave is a building with zero build cost, so week one has something to climb without needing materials the tribe cannot yet make.

**Example surprise** — Night 337: the population map's shape changes. Forty of the valley's sixty-one people are now living within four tiles of the upper gorge, on ground that grows almost nothing, hauling food in from two days away. Later, the river shifts and the gorge silts up, the airflow support drops to 0.3, and over about thirty nights the cluster drains away and the map's centre of gravity moves downstream. **Ceiling or rate** — Both, but unevenly. **How it works** — Each tile gets a terrain support vector — the same 8 axes, holding the maximum value that ground will support — generated from the existing elevation, flow and substrate data by a handful of formulas (airflow from the ratio of local elevation gradient to valley width; motion from flow rate; thermal steadiness from depth below surface for cave tiles; bracing from substrate hardness; wetness from water table). The river's slow meander and its floods recompute terrain support for the tiles they touch, which is how a district loses a rung. **Authored risk** — Low. The terrain-support formulas are yours but they are the same kind of thing as the physics formulas, and crucially they do not encode any technology — 'gorges are windy' is a fact about the landscape, not a recipe. The one thing to avoid: do not hand-place a special tile because it would be nice if there were a good kiln spot. Generate everything from elevation and flow.

**Build cost** — Two or three evenings, because the elevation and flow data already exist in the design. The failure-when-exceeding-support path is another evening. Cheap.

**Tuning burden** — Light but fiddly at the start: the scale factors that turn elevation gradient into an airflow number are arbitrary and you will move them until the map has interesting variation rather than being uniformly mediocre or having one god tile. ### The Three-Budget Audition, and The Shelf  `core` · 9/10

Most invented goals are either trivial or impossible, and both waste the night. So audition every goal before it counts. Try to solve it with a small amount of search. If that works, it was already easy — bin it. Then try again with three times as much search. If that fails too, it is out of reach — put it on the shelf. Only the middle band, can't do it easily but can do it if it really tries, becomes a goal the tribe actually works on. The shelf is not a bin: every night a small slice of compute quietly re-tries shelved goals, and one that has failed sixty times and suddenly succeeds is the loudest, best event this world can produce.

**What becomes possible** — A civilisation that can want something it cannot do. That does not currently exist anywhere in this design — survival goals are either solvable or lethal, so there is no such thing as an aspiration. The shelf is the aspiration, and it is what lets the world tell stories that arc over months rather than nights. It also fixes a quieter problem: when a building unlocks a new capability, today the search only notices if it happens to be looking in that direction. With a shelf, the world has been asking for that exact thing for four months and spots it the night it becomes possible.

**Example surprise** — "NIGHT 388 — A SHELVED WANT WOKE. 'an object with toughness at or above 6.2 that survives 400 steps submerged' — proposed night 41, failed 63 times, solved tonight in 71k nodes. What changed since the last attempt: the soak-pit at Site 91, built night 372, holds a wetness level nothing else in the valley can." That is a 347-night causal chain with a beginning, a middle and an end, and no author anywhere in it.

**Ceiling or rate** — CEILING, directly, and it is the cheapest ceiling in this document. It does not create new capability, but it roughly doubles the fraction of new capability that turns into a noticed, dated, legible event — and for the builder's stated goal, an unexpected event that nobody notices may as well not have happened. More importantly, it systematically biases the big events toward the ones with the longest shelf-life, which is exactly the 'once or twice in six months' shape he said he wanted. The size of the biggest possible surprise here is bounded by how long a want can sit unsolved and then resolve, and that bound is months.

**How it works** — Goal row: {id, predicate (list of column>=value), born_night, state in {audition,live,shelved,retired}, last_tried_night, tries, near_miss_score, first_solved_night, solver_program_id}. AUDITION during the night pass, budgeted in nodes expanded, never in seconds. Run the existing search at B_small (start around 40k nodes, set so the median survival goal solves in roughly that). Solved -> retired, marked trivial, no notification. Not solved -> run at B_big = 3 x B_small. Solved -> live, and its solving program goes immediately into the pile the compressor reads that same night. Not solved -> shelved. THE SWEEP: each night spend a fixed slice (start at 5 percent) of the total node budget re-auditioning shelved goals at B_big only. Order them by one heuristic that turns the sweep from a lottery into a targeted retry: prioritise shelved goals whose predicate mentions a column whose all-time maximum has moved in the last 10 nights, or that mention a site where a works was built or fell down recently. WAKE EVENT: a goal shelved with tries >= 20 that then succeeds is the top-priority push notification, and the payload is the diff — which works exist now that did not at last attempt, which rule instances went live. WHY THE BUDGET RATIO AND NOT A SUCCESS RATE: the standard trick in the curriculum literature is to pick tasks the learner solves sometimes and fails sometimes, scored p times (1-p) — 'Sampling for Learnability', NeurIPS 2024. That does not transfer here, because your search is deterministic: run it twice and you get the same answer, so p is 0 or 1 and p(1-p) is always zero. The budget ratio is the deterministic translation — 'solvable, but only with three times the effort' carries the same information as 'solved about half the time'. Anything you read about learnability needs this substitution before it applies to Glyphworks.

**Authored risk** — LOW, the lowest here. The authored content is three numbers: 40k, the multiplier 3, and the 5 percent sweep slice. None of them decides what gets invented, only what is worth trying. The one honest wrinkle is that the multiplier 3 encodes a taste for 'a modest stretch' over 'a huge stretch'. Run 3 and 10 side by side for a week and look at which shelf produces better wake-ups; that is a measurement, not a preference.

**Build cost** — ~120 lines plus the goal table. It reuses the search you already have, unchanged. Half a day.

**Tuning burden** — B_small must be set once against your actual search speed. The sweep slice is a compute-versus-drama dial: more sweeping means more wake-ups and slower daily progress. Expect to touch both twice in the first month, then never. The genuine trap is the shelf becoming a graveyard of fifty thousand impossible goals that costs real compute every night — cap it at a few thousand and evict by 'never near-missed and mentions no recently-moved column'.

**Interactions** — Every other system's goals go through this, without exception — systems 1, 3, 4 and 5 are all just goal sources. System 6 decides how the live-goal budget gets split. System 3 is what makes shelf wake-ups happen at all, because a new physical law is the most common reason an impossible thing becomes possible. System 4 reads the state field: a goal that everyone solves retires, one nobody solves shelves.

**Chance it does nothing** — About 10 percent — the highest-confidence system here, because it is mostly a filter and filters rarely make things worse. The real risk is not failure but disappointment: if nothing in the world ever raises capability, the shelf just grows and never wakes, and you get a long list of things that will never happen. That is not a bug in this system, it is this system correctly reporting that your ceiling has been hit — which is exactly the diagnostic you want and currently do not have.

### Blanks  `core` · 8/10

Rewrite the physics as about six sentences with holes in them. Something like: when a material whose [property P] is above [value V] is held for [duration D] beside [material class M], its [property Q] changes by [amount A]. A building is then defined as a thing that can hold a particular set of numbers steady in those holes. So a works does not merely make an old rule cheaper — it makes one specific filled-in version of a rule template genuinely live, at that spot, that no bare hand could sustain. After this, the number of distinct live laws is six templates times however many meaningfully different blank settings the built environment can reach, and that second factor is not something you enumerated or can enumerate. The specific consequence he cares about: a chain of buildings where each one makes the next one's blanks physically reachable becomes something the world can want, detect, and announce.

### The Record Book  `core` · 7/10

Right now the valley only ever asks three questions: are you fed, are you warm, did something break. So add a second source of questions that costs nothing to invent. The simulator already runs millions of programs a night; while each one runs, jot down about fourteen numbers describing what physically happened — hottest thing, longest anything stayed hot, hardest object that existed, most different materials in one output, deepest chain of one thing feeding into the next. Keep a list of every genuinely different combination of those numbers that has ever occurred. Each night, take one entry from the outer edge of that list, pick a couple of its numbers, and demand they be beaten by thirty percent. That is a goal nobody wrote: it is just the edge of what has already happened, shoved outward one step.

**What becomes possible** — Technology with no immediate survival payoff. Today a tribe that is warm and fed has literally nothing left to do, which is why the whole thing goes flat in six weeks. With a record book it always has something to chase, and chasing a record usually requires infrastructure whose survival value only shows up two steps later. This is how you get a kiln in a world where nobody happens to be cold this week. It also gives you a single honest number for 'how far has this civilisation actually got' — the size and outer radius of the book — which is worth having for its own sake.

**Example surprise** — Phone, 7am: "NIGHT 212 — NEW RECORD. Held above 810 degrees for 240 steps (previous best 96). Program ran the twin hearth at Site 114 during a ridge wind." **How it works** — READINGS: a struct of ~14 floats accumulated during any program run — peak temperature; longest consecutive steps above each of four temperature bands; max hardness and max toughness of any object that existed; count of distinct materials in the final object; total mass transformed; max carry distance; distinct sites touched; longest an object survived intact; number of works invoked; longest chain where one transformation's output was another's input. **Authored risk** — MEDIUM-HIGH, and this is the sharpest authored seam in the whole set, so do not let it slide. You choose the fourteen readings, and choosing what gets measured is choosing what counts as an achievement. First, derive most columns mechanically instead of by hand: every property that appears in any physics rule template's blanks gets a column automatically, so the book's shape is a function of the rulebook rather than of your taste. Second, stamp each column with the date you added it, and when reviewing progress, discount anything achieved in a column you added last month — otherwise you are marking your own homework.

**Build cost** — ~150 lines. Reading accumulator ~40, the book with its distance check ~50, the goal sampler ~40, glue ~20. A day, maybe two with the tuning.

**Tuning burden** — One real knob: L, the how-different-counts-as-different threshold. Too small and the book fills with near-duplicates and the distance check slows down; too large and it forgets fine structure and stops proposing anything specific. Set it so the book reaches a few thousand entries over the first month, and expect to touch it perhaps three times ever. The 1.3x stretch factor matters less than instinct suggests, because system 2 discards anything that turns out too easy or too hard regardless.

**Interactions** — Feeds goals into system 2 (audition). Its reading machinery is reused by system 3 to work out what blanks a works can hold, and by system 5 to phrase 'you are about to run out of clay' as a properties goal. System 3 feeds it back new columns, which is the loop that matters. System 4's goal mutation picks new clauses from its columns. System 6 clusters over its goals.

**Chance it does nothing** — About 20 percent that it is actively useless. The realistic failure is not silence, it is record-chasing junk: a program throws everything on the fire, sets a peak-temperature record and achieves nothing. Two defences. Only count readings from runs where the actor survived. And more importantly, a record only earns a notification if a chunk of the record-setting program later shows up in some other accepted program — records that lead nowhere still enter the book, they just never wake your phone. If you find yourself lowering that bar to get more notifications, the system has failed and you are papering over it.

### The Forecast  `strong` · 7/10

At the moment the world hits the tribe with a flood and the tribe reacts, which only ever produces recovery, never prevention. Its own stated failure (solve depletion by walking away) is real and correctly diagnosed as a test of Blanks: if buildings are cheap to replace, this system produces migration instead of technology and should not be built at all.

**Legibility** — Very good, and it is the only system that produces an event with a DEADLINE in it. 'Want, deadline night 191, reason: clay exhausted night 191' is a piece of dramatic structure the other five cannot generate — a clock the reader can watch run down. Cheap drama, honestly earned from world state.

**Notes** — ~200 lines, two to three days, and the projection is only cheap because it is stupid — resist every instinct to improve it, since a more accurate forecast predicts extinction constantly and drowns the phone. Build fifth, after Blanks, because its depletion goals are phrased from live rule instances and are thin without them.

### The Gold Rush  `cheap-win` · 5/10

With hundreds of live goals and a fixed number of program attempts per night, most effort lands on goals that were never going anywhere. So keep one number per neighbourhood of goals: how many goals near here were solved in the last ten nights, minus how many in the ten before that. Split the night's effort in proportion to that number. When one region of the goal space suddenly starts yielding — because a building went up and opened a door — the whole valley's attention piles into it within a night or two, and you get a burst of related inventions instead of one lonely one. Survival always takes its share off the top first; this only divides what is left.

**What becomes possible** — The same hardware finds several times as much of what was already possible, which matters because every other system in this list spends compute. Less obviously: the TIMING of invention becomes clumpy instead of smooth. Real technological history is clumpy, and it is clumpy for exactly this reason — an opened door makes many nearby things possible at once and everyone piles in. Clumpy history reads far better on a phone than a steady trickle, because a flare is a story and a trickle is a graph.

**Example surprise** — "NIGHT 333 — BURST. 9 wants solved in 3 nights, all in the 'sustained wet heat' cluster. Trigger: 'steamcairn' at Site 60, night 331." On the map, six sites flare over three nights and then go quiet again for a month. One building, nine inventions, and then silence — and you did not schedule any of it.

**Ceiling or rate** — NO. This is a rate system and I am not going to pretend otherwise. My guess is two to four times more solved goals per night from the same hardware, plus a much better-looking discovery pattern. It makes nothing newly possible. It is here because the rest of the engine will drown in unproductive search without it — six goal sources feeding one search budget is a compute problem before it is a design problem — and because a burst is the cheapest way to make a real event legible on a phone screen.

**How it works** — CLUSTERS: k-means over live goals represented as points (their target values, normalised by the Record Book's column maxima), 20-40 clusters, recomputed weekly. Cheap, good enough, and do not make it fancier. SCORE per cluster: progress = (goals in this cluster solved in the last 10 nights) minus (goals solved in the 10 nights before that). This is a learning-progress signal — the change in solve rate, the same idea as ALP-GMM (Portelas and Oudeyer, CoRL 2019) with the statistical machinery thrown away. ALLOCATION: survival goals take a fixed share off the top, always and unconditionally — that is the lethal floor and it is never negotiable. Split the remainder proportional to max(progress, 0) + epsilon, where epsilon is a flat exploration floor so no cluster ever starves entirely. BURST DETECTION: if any cluster's progress jumps above a threshold, that is a technological burst — worth a notification, and worth colouring on the map at the sites where the solving programs ran.

**Authored risk** — VERY LOW, lower than anything else here. It reallocates effort and never decides what exists or what counts. Its only bias is toward whatever is currently productive, which is a bias, but it is the world's bias rather than yours.

**Build cost** — ~80 lines. An afternoon.

**Tuning burden** — One knob that matters: epsilon, the exploration floor. Too small and the engine mines one seam forever and never notices that a door opened somewhere else, which is the exact opposite of what you want. Start it at 20 percent of the divisible budget spread evenly and revisit only if you see the same cluster winning for a month straight.

**Interactions** — Divides the budget that system 2's auditions and live goals consume. System 5's deadline goals get a priority multiplier here. System 3 is the usual cause of a burst, so the burst notification should always name the works and the rule instance that triggered it — that pairing is most of the readable drama in this whole engine.

**Chance it does nothing** — About 15 percent, and the failure is benign: if solve rates are flat everywhere, allocation proportional to progress is uniform allocation with extra steps, and you have lost eighty lines and nothing else. The real risk is not that it does nothing but that it overfits — piling everything into one seam and stalling. That is what epsilon is for, and it is why I would not skip the floor to squeeze out more solves.

### The Spite Engine  `later` · 5/10

Run several tribes, and treat the goals themselves as a second population that also lives, breeds and dies. A goal earns the right to have children only if at least one tribe has solved it and at least one has not. A tribe earns the right to seed a daughter settlement only if it has solved goals no other tribe has solved. That is the entire rule. It sounds too simple to do anything, and it produces something you cannot get any other way: being different from your neighbours is structurally rewarded, because being the same is what kills your right to expand. Tribes go down different technological roads, and when they meet — trade, a raid, a wanderer — you get combinations no single road would ever have reached.

**What becomes possible** — Divergence with an actual reason behind it. Today, four tribes in one valley face identical problems and converge on identical answers, so the second, third and fourth tribes contribute nothing but a bigger sample size. Here the valley grows specialists, and specialists are the precondition for the exact event he said he most wants. 'A building that can only be made from a material another building produces' is dramatically more likely to appear across two lineages that were pushed apart than inside one lineage that was pushed forward.

**Example surprise** — "NIGHT 274 — RAID. Upper-valley tribe took 3 named chunks from lower-valley tribe." Then: "NIGHT 281 — 'blackcairn' built at Site 12. First works whose material requirements can only be met by the output of another works." Two roads, one raid, industry — and the two halves were being developed in parallel by tribes that had a structural reason not to duplicate each other's work.

**Ceiling or rate** — CEILING, specifically for the big rare surprises rather than the small frequent ones. It adds no new physics, so it does not extend the rulebook. What it does is multiply the number of distinct technological states existing simultaneously in one world, and the biggest events in this design are recombinations of distant states. One tribe's stack is a path. Four divergent stacks are four paths plus every crossing between them, and crossings are where the once-in-six-months events live.

**How it works** — This is Minimal Criterion Coevolution (Brant and Stanley, GECCO 2017) — two populations that each earn reproduction by satisfying one condition relative to the other, with no fitness function and no difficulty knob anywhere. Add goal.solved_by, a bitset over tribes, and goal.age. NIGHTLY, per live goal: popcount(solved_by) == 0 for more than N nights -> shelved. popcount == number of tribes -> retired. Otherwise -> REPRODUCTIVE, may spawn one child. CHILD GOAL = parent's predicate with one number perturbed by x1.2 or x0.85, or one clause added (a random Record Book column at a value some tribe has already reached), or one clause dropped. Children enter audition (system 2). TRIBES: each tribe counts goals it solved that no other tribe has. Above K, it may seed a daughter settlement at an unoccupied site, splitting population and copying only a limited number of its named chunks — a hard carry limit, borrowed from Palimpsest's transmission bottleneck, so a big library is a liability for a small daughter and knowledge can genuinely be lost in the move. Counter resets. Cap the goal population at a few thousand; over cap, cull oldest among goals whose solved_by has not changed in a long time. Rate-limit births to a fixed number per night, choosing among reproductive goals by how recently their solved_by changed. WHY THIS RATHER THAN A DIFFICULTY SETTING: the two populations tune each other. Tribes improve, so goals everyone can do retire and only harder children survive. Tribes stall, so goals nobody can do shelve and what remains sits exactly at the edge. Self-balancing, for about eighty lines.

**Authored risk** — LOW. The rules are two 'at least one' conditions and a mutation operator that perturbs numbers. There is no taste in it anywhere. The single leak is which columns a child goal may add a clause about, and those come from the Record Book, so that risk is already counted against system 1 rather than being new.

**Build cost** — ~120 lines, genuinely small. The daughter-settlement mechanics may already exist in the base design. Two days.

**Tuning burden** — Three forgiving numbers: N (nights before a never-solved goal shelves), K (unique solves before a tribe may split), and the population cap. The real thing that will need attention is goal population explosion, since every reproductive goal wants a child every night — the per-night birth cap handles it, but you will get the cap wrong once.

**Interactions** — Consumes goals from systems 1, 3 and 5 and mutates them. Its shelve/retire decisions overlap with system 2's, so pick one owner for the state field — make system 2 own state transitions and let this system only supply the solved_by evidence, or you will get two pieces of code fighting over one column, which is exactly the kind of silent bug he cannot afford. Raiding and trading must actually move named chunks between tribes or the whole point is lost.

**Chance it does nothing** — About 25 percent. The failure mode is concrete and testable in week one: tribes fail to diverge because the valley is too uniform, so solved_by is almost always all-or-nothing, almost no goal is ever reproductive, and the engine idles. Plot the distribution of popcount(solved_by) early. If it is bimodal at zero and maximum, your tribes are not different enough yet and you need real separation first — distance, different mineral strata under different reaches of the river — before this system has anything to bite on.

<details><summary><b>Critic</b></summary>

**Ceiling or rate** — Claims ceiling-via-recombination. The claim is half-right and the half that is wrong is the expensive half. IT FIGHTS BLANKS AND IT FIGHTS THE ONE EVENT HE MOST WANTS, and nobody in this document notices: N tribes on fixed hardware means each tribe gets 1/N of the nightly node budget, and enumerative search buys depth roughly logarithmically in budget. 'A works built from a material only another works can produce' is a DEPTH event — it needs one lineage to get far enough up its own stack to have a second-order need at all. Splitting the budget four ways to buy breadth makes the six-month moment LESS likely, not more, unless cross-lineage transfer is genuinely cheap. It buys recombination breadth at the direct cost of the stacking depth that recombination has to recombine.

**Authored seams** — Genuinely low — two 'at least one' conditions and a numeric perturbation operator, with the only content leak (which columns a child goal may cite) already charged to the Record Book. Credit where due: this is the least opinionated system in the set.

**Will it work** — Not as specified. Two problems. (1) THE MECHANISM DOES NOT PRODUCE DIVERGENCE. MCC's minimal criteria are reproduction filters on the GOAL population; nothing here penalises two tribes for solving the same thing — being second is merely unrewarded, not costly, so the cheapest route stays the cheapest route for everyone. Brant and Stanley hit exactly this and the published fix is RESOURCE LIMITATION (GECCO 2020): give each goal a limited number of solve slots, retire it once C tribes have taken them, and being second becomes actively worthless. One line, cited, and it converts 'hoping for divergence' into 'structurally forcing it'. (2) IT SILENTLY DEPENDS ON A SUBSTRATE CHANGE NOBODY HAS PROPOSED. Divergence requires different AFFORDANCES, not different goals — identical physics plus identical primitives yields identical cheapest solutions. The proposal concedes this in its own failure section ('you need real separation first — different mineral strata under different reaches of the river') and then files it as a footnote. It is a prerequisite, and it is not on anyone's build list. Its own week-one diagnostic is correct and should gate the whole build: plot the distribution of popcount(solved_by); bimodal at 0 and max means the engine has nothing to bite on.

**Legibility** — Good when it fires — a raid that moves named chunks is the most human-readable event in the whole engine, and 'two roads, one raid, industry' is a genuinely great notification. But this is also the system most likely to produce a beautiful notification about a coincidence, because 'these two tribes diverged' is unfalsifiable without a divergence metric he has not been asked to build.

Build it when the shelf has stopped waking for a month — that is the honest signal that one lineage has plateaued and breadth is finally worth more than depth. One piece is worth stealing IMMEDIATELY and separately: the carry-limited daughter settlement borrowed from Palimpsest's transmission bottleneck. Knowledge that can be LOST in a move is drama for about twenty lines and needs none of the coevolution machinery.

### The Ask Board  `core` · 8/10

When somebody's search fails, you currently throw away the failure. Instead, let the search cheat: give it three fake free commands — 'assume I have this material', 'assume this building is in reach', 'assume I know this chunk' — each usable at most once per program. If a program only solves the goal by cheating, you have just learned, mechanically and without anyone writing it down, exactly what that person is missing. Post it as a public request with a price on it. Other people's searches now have a task list made of other people's failures, and the price on an unfilled request rises a few percent every night until somebody earns it.

**What becomes possible** — The world stops needing you to supply problems. Every solved problem creates new missing pieces, each of which is a fresh goal stated in the tribe's own growing vocabulary rather than in your five survival predicates. It also gives the search a budget allocator that nobody authored: an old, expensive, unfilled need slowly becomes worth an enormous search budget, so the world can eventually afford a program far longer than any nightly budget would allow. And it makes trade real without any trade rules — filling a request moves a number between two people, and people who fill a lot of requests can buy labour and teaching.

**Example surprise** — "Kettlemouth has been asking for 'something that holds heat all night' for 61 nights. Price now 41,300. Tonight Sedge, 17, filled it — a program nine steps longer than anything anyone in the valley has ever run, paid for by two months of accumulated bounty." The world posted a bounty on a problem nobody wrote down, and the bounty is what made the answer affordable.

**Ceiling or rate** — CEILING, directly, and this is the strongest ceiling argument in my set. Two reasons. First, the price rise turns your nightly search budget into an accumulating one: something unaffordable tonight becomes affordable in fifty nights, so the depth limit stops being 'what fits in one night' and becomes 'what a whole civilization is willing to spend', which is a much higher number. Second, the audit's nastiest ceiling was that cheap buildings make the search lazy and the tribe ossifies in a local rut — a rising unfilled price is precisely the counter-pressure, because it keeps pointing money at the thing that is still not solved.

**How it works** — In the search, add three pseudo-commands: `assume-have(material)`, `assume-works(type)`, `assume-know(chunk)`. Each costs a large fixed number of search steps and at most one may appear in any candidate — that cap is essential or wishes cascade and every problem is solved by a miracle. When the best program for a goal uses a wish, write `request(id, requester, kind, target_id, offered, posted_tick, expiry)` with `offered` seeded from the goal's urgency (nights until the person starves or freezes, inverted). Dedupe by (tribe, kind, target) keeping the max price, cap the board at 200 rows, evict cheapest. Every night: `offered *= 1.06` for unfilled rows, and expire at 400 nights. Requests within a person's reach (same site, adjacent site, or a trade partner within N tiles) enter that person's task list, and the search budget for a task is allocated proportional to sqrt(offered), so a huge bounty gets a big but not infinite share. Filling a request writes a row to `favour(from, to, amount, tick)`; running totals of favours are what buy labour ticks and teaching slots elsewhere. The whole thing is one table, one nightly UPDATE, and a change to how the search picks what to work on.

**Authored risk** — Medium, and it is concentrated in three numbers: the wish prices, the 6% nightly rise, and how urgency maps to a starting price. Those three decide which problems the civilization takes seriously and for how long, which is real influence. What you do NOT author is what anyone wants — the wants come out of actual search failures on actual goals, so the content of the board is downstream of the physics. The honest worry is you will discover the 6% is really 3% or 12% and spend a week finding out, and during that week you are choosing the world's ambition level by hand.

**Build cost** — Moderate — the biggest of the six. The wish-token relaxation inside the search is fiddly to get right (tracking which wish a candidate used, refusing to let one wish enable another, keeping the answer bank correct across wished and unwished programs). Call it 600-900 lines plus a favour ledger. Everything else is a SQLite table and a cron-style nightly pass.

**Tuning burden** — Real but bounded. Expect one week of watching whether the board fills with 200 rows all asking for firewood (fix: dedupe harder, and let requests be for chunks and buildings, not just materials) or stays empty (fix: cheaper wish tokens). After that it self-runs, and it actually REMOVES a tuning knob, because search budget allocation across tasks stops being a number you pick.

**Interactions** — Feeds The Winnowing (price is the only honest input to 'what should we teach this season'), feeds The Walking Out (a person's earnings on the board is the number that decides who leaves), and pays for Deeds and Ruins (a captured building you cannot run generates an immediate, loud, rising request for the chunk that runs it). It also doubles as your junk-chunk detector, which is the audit's 'the naming model manufactures fake depth' problem: a chunk that is never requested, never taught, and never priced is plumbing, no matter how well it compressed or how good a name Qwen gave it.

**Chance it does nothing** — About 25% that it degenerates — the board converges on the same five materials forever and reads like a shopping list. The guard is that requests can name chunks and building types, which are open-ended sets that grow with the library. If after a month more than 80% of board value is material requests, the wish tokens are mispriced and you should make `assume-know` and `assume-works` cheaper than `assume-have`.

**Ceiling or rate** — CEILING, and it is the only one of the six aimed at the problem every audit across 42 designs independently named: the supply of problems. But it raises the ceiling less than claimed and in a different place. Do it as a relaxed plan instead. Polynomial, runs entirely outside the enumerator, never touches the bank, and returns a RANKED set of missing preconditions rather than one wish. Strictly better on every axis. Second unaddressed engineering item: 'budget accumulates over 50 nights' only helps if the search for that request is RESUMABLE — you need a checkpointed priority-queue frontier per request, and that frontier goes stale when the world changes under it, which is the same cache-coherence problem again. Version-key the frontier or you get expensive wrong answers on your most important goals.

**Legibility** — High and unusually good, because the board is a sorted list of what the world wants and cannot get — that is a genuine 30-second check-in that is not a map. 'Kettlemouth has been asking for something that holds heat all night for 61 nights, price 41,300' is a headline. The failure mode is legible too: if the top 20 rows are all firewood you can see it in four seconds.

Add one more: the board is the natural home for the thing that is actually missing from all six proposals — a request whose target is an EMPTY CELL in a MAP-Elites archive over the material property space. Emberlisp's audit and Palimpsest's audit independently prescribed exactly this (auto-curriculum / POET minimal criterion) and both said it was ~80-150 lines. With the property vectors and the fingerprint table already built, emitting 'produce something landing in this unoccupied region' costs you almost nothing and converts the board from a decomposer of five fixed goals into a frontier that recedes as the tribe advances. That single addition is worth more than proposals 3, 5 and 6 combined.

### The Shown and the Hidden  `core` · 8/10

Two things. First, people learn to withhold: if teaching a chunk historically cost the teacher income, the chance they teach it again goes down, and after a while some chunks are secrets held by two or three people. Second, and much more important: you cannot hide a RESULT. A pot sits in someone's hands with a measurable property record; a kiln stands on a tile in a measurable physical state. So a person who sees a thing they cannot make gets a goal that is specified by the object itself — 'produce something whose properties land within this distance of that' — and has to find their own program for it. The target is guaranteed to be reachable, because somebody reached it.

**What becomes possible** — A supply of problems set at precisely the frontier, forever, with no designer input at all. Every goal of this type is known-achievable (someone did it) and known-hard (you can't do it), which is the exact band the search is most productive in, and the band moves as fast as the fastest tribe in the valley. It also means the world accumulates MULTIPLE ROUTES to the same effect, because a reverse-engineered program is almost never the original, and different routes have different ingredients, so they branch differently later. And it gives the search something it badly needs: a distance to climb, instead of a yes/no answer.

**Example surprise** — "Underhang bought black jars from Kettlemouth for 200 nights. On night 730 an Underhang potter made one from a different program — three steps longer, river silt instead of ash — and it holds water better than the original. Kettlemouth's jar trade died in twelve nights." Nobody wrote a spy, a trade war, or an industrial secret; a woman looked at a pot and worked out her own way to make one.

**Ceiling or rate** — CEILING, and it is the piece I would build first if you only built one. It removes you from goal-setting entirely for the hardest goals: the target vector comes from another tribe's actual achievement, so there is no list of predicates to exhaust. Separately it raises the practical ceiling on search depth, because a 'get near this property vector' goal lets you RANK half-finished programs by how close they got, where a boolean survival predicate gives you no signal at all until you win. That partial-credit ranking is the standard answer to 'past three layers deep, blind search stalls'.

**How it works** — Withholding: per (person, chunk) keep `share_p` starting at 0.9. Forty nights after a teaching event, compare the teacher's favour income attributable to that chunk in the 40 nights after versus the 40 before; if it fell, share_p *= 0.8, if it rose, share_p *= 1.05, clamp to [0.02, 0.98]. Two lines, and guilds either appear or they don't. Artifact targets: whenever a person observes an object or a building state they cannot produce, emit `task(kind='match', target_vector, tolerance d, bounty)` where target_vector is the observed property record. The search scores partial programs by Euclidean distance in the (normalised) property space and best-firsts on it; success is distance < d. Rediscovery and independent-invention detection, which you want anyway: give every chunk a PROBE FINGERPRINT — run it from 32 fixed sample world-states, take the resulting state changes, and set one bit per feature that moved past a threshold, giving a 64-bit integer (this is SimHash-style fingerprinting, the same trick used for near-duplicate web pages). Two chunks are 'the same technology' if their fingerprints are within Hamming distance 3 — one XOR and a popcount. That single table gives you, free: independent invention (matching fingerprints, no shared ancestry), rediscovery (a fingerprint that went extinct N nights ago reappearing with a different body), and successful reverse-engineering (a new fingerprint matching the producer of an artifact someone was staring at).

**Authored risk** — Low, and this is the cleanest of the six. The tolerance d and the share_p constants are yours, but the GOALS are not — a target vector is a reading off a real object made by a real program, so you cannot bias which technologies become targets without biasing the physics itself. The one seam worth watching: the choice of which property dimensions go into the distance function is a choice about what counts as 'the same thing', and if you weight hardness heavily you will get a world obsessed with hard things.

**Build cost** — Moderate, but the money is well spent. The fingerprint table is trivial (32 probe runs per chunk, once, at birth). The distance-goal search mode is the real work — a distance function over property records plus a best-first ordering — call it 400-600 lines, and it makes the whole search better at everything, not just this. The share_p bookkeeping is a hundred lines.

**Tuning burden** — Light. Mostly the tolerance d: too loose and every muddy lump counts as a copy of the black jar, too tight and reverse-engineering never succeeds. Set it by measuring — take pairs of chunks you personally judge 'the same technology' and pick d that separates them. Half a day, once.

**Interactions** — Needs Heads Not Shelves (secrets are meaningless if knowledge is a shared table) and pairs with Deeds and Ruins (a captured building you cannot run is the single best source of artifact-targets, since it sits there in a physical state you can measure and cannot reproduce). The fingerprint table is also what makes The Walking Out's reunions legible, since it tells you when two lineages independently reached the same place. It gives The Ask Board a second kind of thing to price.

**Chance it does nothing** — Low, about 15%, and the failure would be that the property space is too small — if your materials have eight fields with narrow ranges, artifact targets are so coarse that everything matches everything and the whole thing collapses into 'make a hard thing'. ### Heads Not Shelves  `core` · 7/10

Right now the tribe's list of named chunks is one shared table — anyone can run anything. Change it so each named chunk lives in specific people's heads, with a skill number attached, and each head only holds about thirty chunks' worth before the least-practised one falls out. If the last person who still knows a chunk dies, that chunk is deleted for real: its body is gone from the runnable set, and every chunk whose body called it is marked broken, with the reason chain printed. The tribe wakes up unable to do things it did yesterday, and you get a notification that reads exactly like a stack trace.

**What becomes possible** — Knowledge stops being free and infinite and becomes a thing that is somewhere, held by someone, and losable. Three whole classes of problem exist that could not exist before: getting a person to a place so they can be taught, keeping a fragile skill alive when only one old woman has it, and re-deriving something the tribe used to be able to do. It also puts a hard cap on how deep one person's stack of skills can go, which means depth past that cap can only be reached by several people each holding a part and handing off — so programs that coordinate people become worth searching for. Without a head limit, depth is just one brain knowing more and nobody ever has to organise anything.

**Example surprise** — "Night 611. Ithra, 54, died crossing the ford. `bank-the-coals` is extinct — she was the last. 7 chunks broke. Longest broken chain: sealed-jar <- glaze-slip <- fired-clay <- bank-the-coals. The tribe can no longer store water through winter." Then forty nights later a different person gets water storage working again by a route with no fire in it at all, because the fire route is unavailable and the search had to go somewhere else.

**Ceiling or rate** — Mostly RATE and variance, and I will not pretend otherwise — losses and re-derivations are a steady supply of drama, not a bigger ceiling. But there is one real ceiling effect and it is the head limit. Cap a head at ~30 chunks and any technology needing a deeper stack than that is physically unholdable by one person, so getting past it requires handing work between people, which is a new KIND of program to invent (meet, wait, hand over, signal). Physics alone never forces that. So the limit converts 'depth' into 'organisation', and organisation is a fresh direction with no authored top.

**How it works** — Tables: `person(id, tribe, site, age, calories, known_set_version)`; `knows(person_id, chunk_id, fluency REAL, last_used_tick)`; `chunk(id, body, name, base_cost, parents[])` where parents are the chunks its body calls. A person may only run a program if every named chunk in it is in their `knows` with fluency > 0.2. Fluency multiplies run cost (cost = base_cost / fluency) and gives a mangle chance of (1 - fluency) * 0.3, where mangling drops one random step at run time. Fluency: -0.002 per night unused, +0.05 per successful use, clamped to [0,1]. Head capacity: sum over known chunks of weight = 1 + depth_in_dependency_graph must stay under CAP (start at 30); on overflow, drop the lowest fluency. Death: when no living person has fluency > 0 in chunk C, mark C extinct at this tick, delete its body from the runnable set, then walk the reverse-parent edges marking every caller BROKEN with a reason chain (keep broken chunks in the table so the trace stays readable and so a later rediscovery can be matched against them). Runs at the moment of death, once, on a graph of a few hundred nodes — microseconds. The search fix for stale remembered answers: search now runs per person, so key the answer bank by (goal, site, works-bitset, person.known_set_version), and bump that one integer whenever the person learns, forgets, or a chunk goes extinct. That is a cleaner handle on the stale-answer bug than anything site-based, because knowledge changes are already discrete events you can hook. To stop searching once per person, hash each person's sorted chunk-id list into a class id and search once per class — in practice a tribe of forty has three to eight distinct classes.

**Authored risk** — CAP, the fluency curves, the decay rate, and 'weight = 1 + depth' are all your numbers and they set how hard the world is. That is real authorship of DIFFICULTY. But it is not authorship of CONTENT: nothing here decides what gets invented, only how easily it survives. The genuine trap is that you will tune CAP until the world keeps roughly the amount of technology you find pleasing, which is you steering the outcome by feel. Guard against it by fixing CAP once and never touching it again after week three — write it down as a promise to yourself.

**Build cost** — Small. Two tables, a version integer, a reverse-edge walk, and the fluency arithmetic — call it 400-600 lines. The real cost is not lines, it is that the search is now per-knowledge-class instead of per-tribe, which multiplies search work by three to eight. Budget for that up front, since throughput is already the sore spot.

**Tuning burden** — Two evenings, then leave it. CAP and the death rate together decide whether the world accumulates anything at all; get them wrong and you either get permanent scrabble (nothing survives long enough to build on) or nothing ever gets lost. One safety rail worth building on day one: a chunk with four or more living knowers is effectively immortal, so the danger only applies to new and specialist knowledge, which is where the drama should be anyway.

**Interactions** — This is the foundation the other five stand on. The Ask Board needs 'who knows what' to price teaching. The Winnowing needs head capacity to make choosing what to keep a real choice. Deeds and Ruins needs it so a seized building can be useless to its captors. It fights with throughput: per-person search costs more, and the known-set-class trick is what keeps that from tripling your night.

**Chance it does nothing** — Very low that it does nothing — the first extinction notification lands within days and it is genuinely good. Maybe 30% chance it does something WORSE than nothing for a month, by making the world so lossy that no stack ever gets past depth three. That is a tuning failure, not a design failure, and the four-knowers rail mostly prevents it.

### The Winnowing  `strong` · 6/10

Once every twenty-four nights, teaching happens, and there are far fewer teaching slots than there are chunks worth teaching. So the tribe has to pick, and what it picks is whatever is expensive on the Ask Board and whatever only one or two people still know. Everything below the line just doesn't get passed on — and if its holders die first, it's gone. Because two neighbouring tribes have different buildings, different ground and different demands, they winnow different things, and after a couple of hundred nights they are two groups who each forgot half of a shared inheritance.

**What becomes possible** — A big library becomes a liability rather than a trophy, which is the opposite of how these systems normally behave and it is immediately visible. More importantly, tribes DIVERGE in what they know while both descending from the same stack, which makes a genuinely new kind of thing possible: a building that no single group can put up, because the recipe needs one chunk only Kettlemouth kept and one chunk only Underhang kept. Let a build be executed by a party of people rather than one person, pooling their known chunks, and joint projects between groups exist without anyone writing a diplomacy system.

**Example surprise** — "Night 892. `terrace-weir` was built at site 214 by four people — two from Kettlemouth, two from Underhang. Neither tribe has been able to build it alone since night 700, when Kettlemouth stopped teaching `channel-cut` and Underhang stopped teaching `wet-pack`." A building that exists only because two groups forgot different things and had to stand on the same tile.

**Ceiling or rate** — CEILING, clearly. Head capacity caps one person; the teaching budget caps one tribe's total holdings; pooling across groups is the only route past both. So the maximum complexity of anything buildable stops being a fixed number and becomes a function of how many distinct groups can be brought to one place at one time — an axis with no top you wrote. It also raises the ceiling a second way, through copy errors: a failed teaching leaves a mangled body, and a mangled body is free variation the compressor sees the next night.

**How it works** — Every 24 nights, run a teaching round. Each person gets teaching slots = floor(calorie surplus over the last season / S), typically 0-3. Each teaching event costs teacher and learner K co-located ticks doing nothing else. Success chance p = teacher_fluency * 0.9^(nodes_in_body / 8), so long programs transmit badly — this is the single most important line in the system. On success the learner gets fluency 0.4 in that chunk; on failure they get a MANGLED private copy at fluency 0.3 with one step randomly deleted or duplicated, which is a real variant that either works or doesn't. Which chunks get taught: greedy pick, highest `value` first until slots run out, where value(C) = current Ask Board price for C, plus a large constant if living knowers of C <= 1. Nothing else in that formula — keep it purely economic so the curriculum is downstream of real search failures rather than your taste. Track `overlap(A,B) = |chunks(A) ∩ chunks(B)| / |chunks(A) ∪ chunks(B)|` nightly, one SQL query, and push a notification when a pair that used to be above 0.8 drops below 0.4. Builds become party-executable: a build attempt at a site pools the known-chunk sets of everyone standing there, so a party can run a program none of them could run alone.

**Authored risk** — Medium-high, and it sits in exactly one place: the value formula. That formula decides what a civilization considers worth keeping, and if you start adding terms to it ('and things with many callers', 'and things that are beautiful') you are writing the culture by hand. Keep it to price plus the endangered-species bonus and it stays honest, because price traces back to real failures. The slot counts and the 0.9^(n/8) transmission curve are difficulty knobs, same category as CAP.

**Build cost** — Small-to-moderate. A seasonal pass, a greedy pick over a couple hundred rows, a mangle function, one overlap query, and letting a build pool a party's knowledge. 400-600 lines. Party builds are the only piece that touches the search, and only lightly — it is a union of two sets before the search starts.

**Tuning burden** — One or two passes. The failure modes are symmetric and both easy to spot: tribes never diverge (too much teaching, too much trade — cut slots) or they diverge instantly and share no base at all (too few slots — the overlap number goes to near zero in the first hundred nights and stays there). Watch the overlap curve for a fortnight; it should drift down slowly and unevenly.

**Interactions** — Depends entirely on Heads Not Shelves for capacity and on The Ask Board for the price signal — without the board you would have to write the curriculum yourself, which is the whole thing this is trying to avoid. Feeds The Walking Out (divergence makes reunions meaningful) and The Shown and the Hidden (a chunk you refuse to teach and a chunk you cannot afford to teach look the same from outside, which is nice).

**Chance it does nothing** — Maybe 20% it does nothing interesting, and the failure mode is specific: if tribes are in constant contact they re-homogenise faster than the winnowing separates them, and you get one uniform culture with a slightly smaller library. Geography is the fix — the valley needs stretches that are genuinely expensive to cross. If your 400 sites are all within easy walking distance of each other, this system cannot work and you should widen the map before you build it.

**Ceiling or rate** — Mixed, and the headline ceiling claim deflates on contact. 'Pooling across groups is an axis with no top you wrote' is false: pooling is a set union, and a union tops out at the whole valley's library, which is exactly the shared table you started with. So the mechanism adds a COST to reaching the status quo — real drama, real logistics, genuinely good stories — but the same ceiling. The actual ceiling contribution is the part he mentions in one sentence at the end: failed teaching leaves a MANGLED body at fluency 0.3. Palimpsest's audit is explicit that blind subtree mutation is credit-free GP that stalls at single-digit node counts; mutation of verified survivors does not have that problem. The overlap(A,B) Jaccard query with a push when a pair drops from >0.8 to <0.4 is the correct instrumentation and it makes an invisible process into a notification. ### The Walking Out  `strong` · 6/10

When a settlement gets expensive to live in — upkeep eating everyone's time, too many walking trips, too many needs going unmet — a group leaves. The clever bit is WHO leaves: sort people by how much they have earned on the Ask Board recently and the bottom of that list is who walks. So the founders of the new place are exactly the people whose skills nobody at home wanted, carrying those skills to somewhere with different rock, different buildings and different neighbours, where they might be the most valuable thing going. Centuries later two settlements from the same ancestor meet again, having forgotten different halves of the same inheritance.

**What becomes possible** — Knowledge gets transported by a biased sample rather than a random one, which means unwanted skills systematically end up in new places — a mechanism for the outcast's useless trick becoming the founding technology of somewhere else. New settlements also start with empty ground, no buildings and therefore cheap search again, so they can explore routes that the built-up parent settlement has priced itself out of ever trying. And reunion after long separation lets two half-stacks recombine, which is the only way to reach combinations that no single line of development would ever assemble.

**Example surprise** — "Night 1,102. A splinter that left Kettlemouth on night 604 with six people and eleven chunks has come back with three chunks Kettlemouth never had, one of which needs a mineral that only exists on the far terrace. Within thirty nights Kettlemouth was sending people forty tiles to fetch it, and the far terrace has a name now." A whole trade route exists because six people nobody valued walked out five hundred nights ago.

**Ceiling or rate** — CEILING, by two distinct routes, and I think this is under-rated. First, it is the direct antidote to the ossification ceiling the audit named — cheap infrastructure makes the search lazy, but a fresh settlement has no infrastructure at all, so the local cost landscape is different and the search there goes places the old settlement cannot afford to reconsider. Second, divergence followed by reunion is a combination engine: two stacks that developed apart for four hundred nights are far more different than two branches of one stack, and their recombination reaches things neither line would.

**How it works** — Trigger, measured not authored: for each settlement compute `strain = (total works upkeep ticks + total travel ticks + count of expired unfilled requests) / population`, averaged over a 20-night window. Cross a threshold and a splinter forms. Who leaves: rank residents by favour income over the last 40 nights, ascending; each of the bottom third leaves with probability proportional to (1 - their normalised income), so the least-in-demand are most likely to go, and they take exactly what is in their heads — which after The Winnowing is a specific and lopsided set. Where they go: score candidate sites by `-distance + 2*(unclaimed or ruined works within 3 tiles) + material_variety_not_present_at_home`, take the argmax within walking range. Lineage: every settlement carries `founded_from` and `founded_tick`. When two settlements sharing an ancestor come into contact after 150+ nights apart, compute their chunk-set overlap and, if it is under 0.5, push a REUNION notification and run an unrestricted teaching round between them (ignore the seasonal slot limit for one round only — this is the burst that makes reunions matter) plus flag every joint build now newly possible. All of this is a threshold, a sort, and a scoring line.

**Authored risk** — Medium-low. The strain threshold and the destination score are yours, and the destination score in particular is you saying what makes a place attractive. But nobody authors who leaves or what they carry — that is read off the economy, which is read off search failures, which is read off the physics. The honest wobble is that you will tune the strain threshold until splits happen at a rate that looks nice on the map, which is aesthetic steering.

**Build cost** — Smallest of the six. A rolling average, a weighted sort, a site score, a lineage column, and a reunion query. 250-350 lines.

**Tuning burden** — Light but real: one number, the strain threshold, and its two failure modes are obvious on the map (constant splitting into unviable hamlets, or one enormous settlement that never splits). Watch settlement count over the first 300 nights; you want it drifting up, with some splinters dying.

**Interactions** — Needs The Ask Board to know who is unwanted (without it you are picking founders at random and losing most of the value), and needs The Winnowing for divergence to be worth reuniting. Feeds Deeds and Ruins (splinters go where the unclaimed buildings are, so old ruins become destinations) and The Shown and the Hidden (reunion is where the fingerprint table earns its keep, telling you which of the two lineages' chunks are the same technology by different routes).

**Chance it does nothing** — Around 35%, the highest of my six, and for an honest reason: splinters mostly die. Six people with a lopsided skill set walking into empty country is a bad bet, and if 90% of them starve you have added noise and a lot of little grey dots. It is still worth building because the 10% that survive produce the biggest single events in the whole world, but do not expect a steady return — this is a lottery-ticket system, which is exactly the kind of thing you said you wanted.

**Ceiling or rate** — CEILING, and one of his two arguments is better than he makes it sound. ### Deeds and Ruins  `later` · 4/10

A building belongs to whoever is standing on it. There is no combat system: if a group has three times as many people at a site as the current holders for a stretch of days, ownership flips, and defending means keeping people parked there instead of foraging or teaching. The good part is that a seized building is dead weight — the kiln only fires for someone who knows the chunk that fires it. So capture doesn't give you technology, it gives you an expensive stone box and a very loud reason to want the knowledge. And a building nobody living can run rots into a ruin whose stored program is still partly readable, with steps missing in proportion to how long it stood empty.

**What becomes possible** — Technology can change hands independently of the people who invented it, which means two independently invented halves of a stack can end up under one roof. That is the single event the whole project is betting on — a building made from a material only another building can produce — and it becomes far more likely when the two buildings do not have to be invented by the same group. It also creates defection as an economic act rather than a scripted event: holding a building you can't run posts a big rising bounty, and bounties pull people across tribal lines. And ruins give a third way to acquire knowledge (dig it up) whose output has a different shape from being taught or from working it out, so the compressor sees three different bodies for one idea.

**Example surprise** — "Night 455: the Fen-folk took the ash-kiln. Nights 456-489: it stood cold — nobody there knew `long-fire`, and the bounty for it climbed to 12,000. Night 491: Orn of Kettlemouth walked forty tiles and moved in. Night 494: the kiln lit." A man sold out his tribe for money, and neither treachery nor money were written into the rules.

**Ceiling or rate** — Honestly, mostly RATE — most of what this adds is a steady flow of good stories. But it has one sharp, specific ceiling effect worth the whole build: buildings are where the deepest technology physically lives, and letting them change hands, be traded, and be inherited raises the PROBABILITY of the craft-becomes-industry moment, because that moment needs two things to exist in one place and this is the mechanism that puts them there without one group having to invent both. If you care most about that one event happening at all, this is the system that moves its odds.

**How it works** — `works` gains `owner_tribe`, `keepers[]` (person ids allowed to run it), `condition REAL`, `last_run_tick`. Access rule: a person may run `use-works` at a site if they are physically there AND (they are a keeper OR no keeper is present). Seizure: if a tribe's headcount at a site exceeds 3x the resident keepers' headcount continuously for K ticks (K around 30), ownership transfers — no fighting, just occupation, which costs the occupier real person-ticks that feed nobody and therefore self-limits. Crucially, `use-works` still requires the runner to KNOW the chunk stored in it, so a seized building immediately posts a request on the Ask Board for that chunk (kind='chunk'), at the works' own lifetime value, rising nightly. It also becomes a first-class artifact target for The Shown and the Hidden: the sim can read the building's sustained physical state (the temperature it holds, the pressure it maintains) and hand that vector out as a goal. Ruins: when a works has no living keeper for 100 nights, condition decays at 3x rate; below 0.2 it becomes a ruin row storing the original body with a damage mask that deletes floor(nights_abandoned / 60) randomly chosen steps. Reading a ruin is a search problem with holes to fill, and the reconstruction almost never matches the original — which the fingerprint table from The Shown and the Hidden will tell you, and that diff is a lovely notification.

**Authored risk** — Low on content, medium on dynamics. The 3:1 ratio, the K ticks and the decay rates are all yours, and together they decide whether this world is peaceful or a permanent war. That is a big tonal decision made by three numbers, so be honest that you are making it. But no technology, no recipe, and no strategy is authored — occupation, defence, and defection all fall out of people standing in places.

**Build cost** — Small. Ownership columns, a headcount check on a timer, a decay rule, and a damage mask. 300-450 lines. The ruin-reading path reuses the hole-filling search that Glyphworks already specifies for decompilation, so it is nearly free if that exists.

**Tuning burden** — Real, and the most likely of my six to need repeated fiddling. The two bad equilibria are 'nobody ever raids because everyone is at subsistence and cannot spare the people' and 'raiding is the dominant strategy and nothing is ever built'. Watch the seizure count per hundred nights; you want it in single digits, not zero and not forty. Expect three or four passes over the first month.

**Interactions** — Needs Heads Not Shelves for the whole point (captured building, missing knowledge), feeds The Ask Board its loudest requests, feeds The Shown and the Hidden its best artifact targets, and is the main reason The Walking Out's splinters have somewhere worth going (unclaimed and heirless buildings). It fights nothing, but it makes the world harsher, so it stacks badly with an already-harsh CAP.

**Chance it does nothing** — About 30%, and the reason is tuning rather than concept — a world of subsistence farmers has no spare labour to occupy anything, so seizures never fire and this reduces to a decay-and-ruins system, which is still worth having but is half the value. Check it early: if by night 300 you have had zero seizures, either your occupation window K is too long or your world is too poor to have politics.

### Records As Work Orders (The Receding Fence)  `core` · 8/10

Every audit across all forty-two designs said the same thing: the world runs out of PROBLEMS in six to ten weeks, not ideas. This turns the measuring stick into the problem generator. Take every blank number in the physics templates — those are your axes. Imagine a shelf of pigeonholes, one hole per combination of 'roughly this hot, roughly this long, roughly this hard'. Each night the world posts goals of the form 'put something in that empty hole next door to a full one', so the fence moves outward exactly as fast as the tribe does and you never wrote a single goal down.

**What becomes possible** — The tribe gets a reason to build things survival does not require — the difference between a civilisation that stops at 'warm enough' and one that keeps pushing. Concretely it makes second-order buildings SOUGHT rather than stumbled upon: if the only way into an empty pigeonhole is a material no bare hand can make, the search goes looking for a building that makes it. That is the exact event he is betting six months on, and this converts it from an accident into a thing the world is actively hunting.

**Example surprise** — Night 340: 'GOAL MET — the empty hole at (very hot, very long, very brittle) is now occupied by a paste made at the south weir. Nothing has ever been in that hole.' Two nights later a works goes up whose build materials include that paste. You never asked for a paste. You asked for an empty pigeonhole to be filled, and the pigeonhole existed only because your rule templates have blanks in them and a kiln stretched one of the blanks last month.

**Ceiling or rate** — Yes, and this is the only one of my six that raises it directly. The reason: the number of pigeonholes is not fixed. When a works fills in a rule template with a constant nobody has held before, it stretches an axis and opens holes that did not exist last month. Goal supply therefore grows with reachable space instead of being a fixed list of survival needs, which converts the audit's 'flat six to ten weeks then sprawl' into a fence that keeps receding. Not infinite — the templates still cap it — but the cap becomes 'how far the numbers can stretch', not 'how many needs the designer wrote'.

**How it works** — AXES: every blank in every rule template, plus two or three structural ones (chain depth, how many distinct materials in a composite). Call it eight to twelve axes. PIGEONHOLES: cut each axis into about six bands, sized so each band holds roughly the same number of past achievements — so the grid rescales itself as the world grows, five lines of code. The full grid is astronomically large so never build it; keep a table holes(bucket_key, best_program_id, best_cost, filled_night) containing only filled ones — a few thousand rows at most. NIGHTLY GOAL EMISSION: for each filled hole, list the immediate neighbours (one axis, one band over). Any neighbour not in the table is a candidate. Rank candidates by how close they are to something already cheap, post the top fifty into the night's task list alongside the survival tasks, retire a goal once it is filled and cheap. THE DIFFICULTY RULE, which is what makes this work at all: keep a goal only if it is currently unsolved at the normal search budget but solvable at about three times that budget. Anything solvable normally is boring; anything unsolvable at 3x is a wall. Test it by running the fifty candidates at 3x on a small slice of the night. BUDGET SPLIT: survival tasks always get their share first — they are the lethal floor. Frontier goals get whatever is left, capped around 30% of the night. Never let a tribe starve chasing records.

**Authored risk** — Lower than it looks, because the axes come from the templates rather than your taste and the holes come from the tribe's own history — you are never saying WHAT to invent, only 'go somewhere nobody has been, on axes the rules already have'. But two seams. (1) You choose the structural axes (chain depth, composite width) and those are taste. (2) The 30% budget split and the 3x difficulty multiplier are numbers you pick, and between them they decide how adventurous the civilisation is. Neither is a recipe book, which is the important difference, but do not pretend they are neutral.

**Build cost** — A week of evenings. Bucketing and neighbour listing are easy. The fiddly part is wiring frontier goals into the existing task queue and budget accounting without letting them crowd out survival, which is where the bugs will be.

**Tuning burden** — The heaviest of my six and I will not pretend otherwise. Budget split, difficulty multiplier, bands per axis, goal ranking — all move behaviour, all by feel, probably a month of touching them. One mitigation that actually works: log 'goals posted / goals met / goals retired as too hard' nightly and put those three numbers on the phone. Near-zero met means the fence is too far out; near-100% met means it is too close. Two numbers tell you which way to turn.

**Interactions** — REQUIRES the rule-template rewrite from the audit's fix #1 — without blanks there are no axes, the grid is fixed, and this degenerates into another thing that plateaus. Feeds System 1 (records get set chasing goals). Fights System 3 for CPU, so schedule them on different cores. Makes System 5's two-witness gate much easier to satisfy honestly, because a frontier goal is a genuinely different kind of problem from 'I am cold'.

**Chance it does nothing** — About 25%. The failure mode is specific: if the axes turn out to be strongly correlated, the grid is effectively one-dimensional, 'an empty neighbouring hole' always means 'a bit hotter', and you have built an expensive way of saying get hotter. You will know inside three weeks by counting how many DISTINCT axes have ever had a record set. If the answer is one or two, the problem is upstream in the physics templates, not here.

**Ceiling or rate** — Genuine ceiling, and the only one here. It attacks the finding all 42 audits converged on — the bottleneck is problem supply, not the compressor — and it does so endogenously: goals are defined relative to what the tribe has already achieved, so they recede as it advances. Crucially the goal space is not fixed, because a works that instantiates a template blank at an unheld constant stretches an axis and creates holes that did not exist. This is minimal-criterion coevolution (Brant & Stanley) / POET's 'not too hard, not too easy' applied to a civilisation instead of a walker, and three separate audits in the pool independently prescribed exactly this fix for other designs. If only one thing on this list gets built after the template rewrite, it is this.

His own per-axis equal-occupancy rescaling does not fix this because it treats axes independently — which is also why his stated 25% failure mode (correlated axes) survives it. The right mechanism is CVT-MAP-Elites (Vassiliades et al., arXiv 1610.05729): fix a niche budget (5k-20k), run k-means over descriptors actually achieved, use the centroids as the holes. Centroids automatically concentrate on the reachable manifold, so correlated axes stop wasting the archive and the grid rescales itself honestly rather than per-axis. Same week of work, and it converts the biggest failure mode into a non-issue. (b) THE SCREENING COST IS UNBUDGETED. 'Run fifty candidate goals at 3x budget on a small slice of the night' is 150 budget-units of screening on top of a 30% frontier allocation, on a machine whose throughput is already the binding constraint (audit #5). Replace the difficulty test with per-niche ABSOLUTE LEARNING PROGRESS: store per niche the best-known solve cost and its history, sample goals proportional to |Δcost| over a trailing window. Zero screening runs, and the literature (IMGEP/ALP-GMM, and MAGELLAN for large goal spaces) is explicit that learning-progress selection beats both novelty and fixed-intermediate-difficulty at exactly this scale. It also deletes his heaviest tuning dial. (c) WHO PAYS. A frontier goal that costs agent labour starves the tribe; a frontier goal that costs nothing is not the civilisation's goal, it is his. Resolve it explicitly: frontier search is DREAMING — free CPU, discovered programs enter the library, but the materials and labour to actually BUILD anything are still paid in-world under survival selection. That keeps the economy honest and removes the crowding-out bug class he predicts will eat his debugging time. It presumes a learned prior that is nowhere in these six proposals.

**Legibility** — Good but needs care. 'GOAL MET — the hole at (very hot, very long, very brittle) is now occupied' is only readable if the axis names are physical. With CVT centroids the hole has no tidy coordinates, so print the nearest named exemplar plus the two axes it most differs on. His three-number phone line (goals posted / met / retired-as-too-hard) is the right instrument and should ship with it.

**Notes** — Highest tuning burden of the six even after the ALP substitution, and he undersells build cost badly: this is 3-6 weeks with debugging, not 'a week of evenings'. It is still the right call, because it is the only item on the list that changes what the world can DO rather than what he can SEE. Hard prerequisite: the template rewrite. ### The Record Book  `core` · 7/10

Right now nothing in Glyphworks can tell you whether last night was a big night or just another Tuesday. The Record Book is a small set of tables that decides mechanically what counts as a first. It works on two levels: a KIND is new when the combination of (which physics rules it uses) x (which classes of material it touches) x (does it need another building's output) has never been seen before; a RECORD is when any number the physics tracks — hottest sustained temperature, longest held state, deepest chain of buildings feeding buildings — passes its all-time high. Everything else is a variant, and variants get counted, not announced. The curve of firsts-and-records per month then becomes the one honest vital sign of the whole project.

**What becomes possible** — The world can answer 'have I stopped?' without you squinting at a map. You get a single ranked queue of candidate surprises instead of five separate alarms. You get the sprawl counter: 'forty new buildings this month, thirty-nine of them variants of two known kinds' — a diagnosis you currently cannot obtain at all. And you get a list of axes worth pushing on, which is what System 2 eats.

**Example surprise** — Morning digest, night 212: 'RECORD — sustained heat 1,340 (previous best 890, night 96, south weir). NEW KIND — a works whose build material only exists as the output of another works. This kind has never been seen.' That is the industry moment he is betting six months on, and it arrives labelled instead of being one more glyph on a map of a hundred glyphs.

**Ceiling or rate** — Directly, only the rate — and really only the OBSERVED rate. A measuring stick does not make things bigger. But it is the precondition for System 2, which does raise the ceiling, because System 2 needs a machine-readable definition of 'somewhere nobody has been' and this is it. Build this first or the ceiling-raiser has nothing to stand on.

**How it works** — Three channels, all cheap. (a) KIND SIGNATURE: when a chunk gets named or a works gets built, compute a row — sorted ids of the rule templates it fills in, sorted list of material classes it reads and writes (class = a coarse bucket, not the exact material), flags for {consumes another works' output, needs a neighbouring works, holds a state across ticks}, and how many buildings deep its supply chain runs. Hash the row. Table kinds(hash, first_seen_night, first_site, example_id, count). New hash = FIRST OF KIND; existing hash = count+1, and that count IS the sprawl number. (b) FRONTIER RECORDS: every blank number in every rule template is an axis. Table records(axis, value, night, works_id, site) — append, never overwrite, because the history is the graph you want to look at. Any works that sustains a value past the current best writes a row. (c) THE UNINSTRUMENTED CHANNEL, for things you never thought to measure: each night build one long row of plain counts — works of each kind per region, agents per region, how much of each material moved between sites, how often each named chunk ran. Divide each column by its own running average and spread so they are comparable. Keep every night's row in one array (400 nights x 400 numbers is nothing). Tonight's oddness = average distance to its twenty nearest past nights. If tonight is in the top 1% of the last ninety, flag it and name in plain text the three columns that contributed most ('agent positions in the south, and clay moving north'). RANKING: one list, not five thresholds. Score everything, sort, top item goes in the guaranteed morning digest; only push an interrupt if an item clears the 99th percentile of the trailing ninety nights; hard cap two interrupts a day.

**Authored risk** — Medium and worth staring at, because what goes into the kind signature decides what the world is ALLOWED to surprise you with. Leave 'holds a state across ticks' out of the signature and a kiln is not a new kind — it is just a hot rock with extra steps. Defence: build the signature mechanically from the rule templates (every template contributes its id and its blanks automatically) rather than hand-listing features you find interesting, and keep channel (c), which is the only part that can flag something your signature has no vocabulary for. Channel (b) is the cleanest of the three: the axes come from templates you already wrote and the numbers come entirely from the tribe.

**Build cost** — A weekend. Four SQLite tables, one hash function, about sixty lines of array maths for channel (c), and an evening for the ranking and digest layer.

**Tuning burden** — Low but real. Two dials: the push threshold, and how coarse the material-class buckets are in the kind signature — too fine and everything is a new kind, too coarse and nothing is. Expect to touch the buckets twice in the first fortnight and then leave them alone.

**Interactions** — Feeds System 2 (its axes and empty pigeonholes become the goals). Feeds System 3 (picks which three inventions are worth an expensive re-run this week). Gates System 5 (nothing gets a name before the Record Book has classified it). Supplies System 6 with every colour and every tick mark on the map. This is the spine; everything else plugs into it.

**Chance it does nothing** — Near zero that the whole thing is useless. But roughly a 30% chance channel (c) is pure noise for the first two months, because a young world has no history to be unusual against, and you will be tempted to rip it out. Keep it — it starts earning around night 100.

**Ceiling or rate** — Rate only, and strictly the OBSERVED rate — a ruler does not make things longer. ### The Honest Map  `strong` · 7/10

A surprise he cannot see did not happen, so presentation is part of the engine, not decoration. Three rules. One: every building is tinted by how much it was actually USED in the last thirty nights — heavily used ones glow, never-used ones render as pale outlines. Two: a strip along the bottom of the map is the Record Book timeline, one pixel column per night, so records appear as ticks you can scrub to. Three: one PNG per night, forever, stitched weekly into a video — that is the hour-long binge and the thing he shows people.

**What becomes possible** — A field of pale outlines is instantly, visually a bug. He can SEE junk buildings without reading a word, which is the single most valuable property in the whole set: the interface tells him when the engine is lying to him. The scrubber lets him ask the only question worth an hour of an evening — 'what was standing here the night before that record was set?' — and get an answer by dragging a finger.

**Example surprise** — Idly scrubbing the time-lapse he notices the south cluster went pale — every building there stopped being used — three nights BEFORE the flood he assumed killed it. Something else killed the south. He never instrumented for that, never wrote a detector for it, and saw it because a colour changed on a map he was scrubbing with his thumb. That is a genuinely unexpected event delivered by a rendering choice.

**Ceiling or rate** — No — only the rate. But specifically the rate of surprises that actually reach him, which is the only rate that counts for his stated goal. It also has the best ratio of surprises-noticed to hours-of-work of anything here, and it is the part of the project he is already expert at.

**How it works** — Nightly render a 1024x1024 PNG from the SAME canvas code the phone page uses — one code path, so the archive and the phone can never disagree. Colours all come from tables that already exist: works tinted by thirty-night call count on a four-step log scale; ruins grey; agents as dots; river blue; a bright outline on any works currently holding an all-time record; a pale hollow outline for zero calls in thirty nights. Tap the strip to jump the map to that night. ### The Replay Rig  `strong` · 6/10

Two questions you currently cannot answer: did that invention actually MATTER, or was it merely new? And is the search's memory lying to me? Both are answered by one piece of machinery — the ability to re-run a stretch of history exactly, with one thing changed. Save a snapshot every night, make every random number come from a seed you control, and then you can go back to night 300, delete one named chunk, and run the next twenty nights with identical weather, identical floods, identical dice, and watch what fails to appear. The same rig, run the other way, catches the bug he is most afraid of.

**What becomes possible** — You get a number for MATTERED, not just for NEW — and 'new but irrelevant' is exactly the trap a compressor sets for you. You also get to catch the audit's scariest bug, the search reusing an answer from a world that no longer exists, on the night it happens rather than six weeks later when the map has quietly gone wrong and there is no error message anywhere.

**Example surprise** — 'SUPPRESSION TEST: remove bank-the-coals (night 96). 14 of the 21 works built since then never get built. Population at night 320 falls from 44 to 19.' That chunk is load-bearing and now you know it. And the equally valuable, deflating one: 'remove fold-twice (night 301, this week's headline invention). Nothing changes at all.' It was decoration, and a system that can tell you that is worth more than one that flatters you.

**Ceiling or rate** — Rate and trust, not ceiling — flatly. It does help the ceiling in two indirect ways worth the money. First, junk chunks that never die eat search budget and clutter the library, and a growing library is what stalls the search past three layers deep; an evidence-backed weekly cull keeps it small enough to still reach. Second, knowing which chunks are load-bearing tells you where a flood or a raid would actually hurt, which turns disasters from scenery into events with consequences.

**How it works** — DETERMINISM FIRST, non-negotiable, and build it on day one because retrofitting it later is genuinely miserable: one random-number generator per site and per agent, seeded by hashing (world seed, site id, night number), never one global generator. Then removing an invention does not shift every dice roll in the valley, and the difference you see is the invention rather than noise — this is the standard 'same dice' trick for comparing two runs of a simulation, and it is the difference between needing one re-run and needing thirty. NIGHTLY SNAPSHOT: whole world state to a compressed file; keep every night for thirty days and every tenth night forever. CANDIDATE PICKING (cheap, nightly): build the graph of 'chunk X's body uses chunk Y' and run the dominator computation on it — Lengauer-Tarjan, an old well-understood algorithm that answers 'which single node does every path to these nodes have to pass through'. A chunk that dominates a big slice of the library is structurally load-bearing. Milliseconds on twenty thousand nodes, and it is how you pick which three chunks deserve an expensive test. WEEKLY SUPPRESSION RUN (expensive): for each of three candidates, restore the snapshot from twenty nights ago, mark that chunk as never-discovered, re-run twenty nights. Report works never built, records never set, population delta, tasks that stopped being solved. Sixty extra simulated nights per week — schedule it when the main loop is not saturating. MEMORY AUDIT (nightly, cheap, the actual bug catcher): the search remembers 'program P in situation S gave result R'. Every night pick 200 remembered entries at random, actually re-run them in the CURRENT world, compare. Any mismatch means your memory is keyed wrong. Put the mismatch count on the phone as a number that should always be zero. A number that should always be zero, displayed every single day, is the cheapest bug detector in existence.

**Authored risk** — Low — this is measurement, not content. The one place taste sneaks in is the scoring formula, how you weigh 'population fell' against 'records never set'. Dodge it: report the raw list for the first month, decide what you actually care about after you have seen thirty of them, and only then collapse it to a single number.

**Build cost** — Determinism and snapshotting is a weekend and must happen on day one. The suppression runner and the memory audit are another weekend. The dominator computation is an afternoon — libraries for it exist and twenty thousand nodes is nothing.

**Tuning burden** — Almost none, which is why it is worth building. Pick how many nights to re-run (start at twenty, raise it if downstream effects have not shown up by then). The audit sample size barely matters; 200 is plenty.

**Interactions** — System 1 supplies the candidate list. System 5 refuses to name anything until it has a MATTERED score, so the one-line note under each name is actually true. System 4 reuses this exact machinery, so building this makes the shadow valley nearly free. Competes with System 2 for CPU.

**Chance it does nothing** — 10% that the suppression test has no resolution, and the reason would itself be interesting: if removing ANY chunk collapses the tribe, everything is load-bearing and the score cannot discriminate. The likelier risk, maybe 35%, is that you build it, love it for a month, then stop reading it because the answer is usually 'yes it mattered'. Keep the memory audit running regardless — that half earns its keep forever.

**Ceiling or rate** — Neither, directly — it is trust infrastructure. Two indirect ceiling effects, one real and one overstated. ### The Two-Witness Naming Gate  `cheap-win` · 6/10

The audit says roughly three in four repeated chunks are plumbing with no human meaning, and a small language model will cheerfully name every one, which manufactures fake depth exactly where there is least. Until then it shows as a grey hash — 'fragment 4417' — and the map draws it as an unnamed grey mark. Names are earned, not issued.

Rarer names are better names.

**Example surprise** — 'Night 155: fragment 2210, unnamed since night 88, has now been used by the north tribe for staying warm and by the river tribe for softening hide. Two witnesses. ### The Shadow Valley  `cut` · 3/10

The entire design rests on one claim: that buildings make new things POSSIBLE, not merely cheaper. You currently have no way of knowing whether that claim is true in your world. So run a second valley on the same machine with the same seed, same weather, same floods, same starting tribes, and exactly one change — in the shadow, a building gives only the cost discount and never fills in a rule template with a new constant. Both valleys sit on the phone. The night they diverge, and by how much, is the answer.

**What becomes possible** — You can finally answer 'is my engine causing anything, or would a plain cheapness discount have produced the same map?' That is the difference between believing the project works and knowing it. It also turns his monthly god-nudge from a guess into an experiment: change a physics template, then watch whether the gap between real and shadow widens. Nothing else here can tell him which change to the rules actually opened new ground.

**Example surprise** — 'Night 180: shadow 31 works, real 34 — basically the same map. Night 260: real valley 51 works in three clusters and one second-order building; shadow 38 works, all one storey, population flat.' Or the far more useful and uncomfortable result: 'Night 400: the two maps are still indistinguishable.' That is a finding. It says the template rewrite is not giving buildings enough new physics, and it says it in a way he cannot argue with at 7am.

**Ceiling or rate** — Not directly. It raises the ceiling on how much he can BELIEVE a surprise, which for this project is nearly as valuable, because his most likely failure is a gorgeous map that means nothing. Indirectly it does raise the real ceiling, by being the only instrument that can tell him which physics change actually bought new ground — which makes his monthly nudges hit instead of scatter.

**How it works** — Same code, one flag in the world config: works_can_instantiate_templates = false. Same world seed, same per-site random streams, same weather schedule, same starting tribes and starting library. Run a THIRD world as the floor: invention_off, where the compressor never runs and tribes only ever use the base commands. Three lines on one chart — floor, shadow, real. ### The Sealed Rulebook  `core` · 9/10

Right now you write the physics: about twelve rules, so about four rungs of technology, and you know all of them before the world starts. Instead, write down the SHAPES a rule is allowed to take (six or so), and let the world's random seed fill in the blanks — which property, what threshold, how long it has to be held, what changes. That gives you two hundred rules instead of twelve, and you have not read any of them. Almost all of them are asleep, because nothing in the valley can yet reach the state that triggers them; a building that can hold a state wakes one up. You stop being the author of your world's laws and become the first person to find out what they are.

**What becomes possible** — The valley can contain a law you never thought of, and the tribe can be the thing that finds it. The number of technology levels stops being something you counted out in advance — it becomes the length of the longest chain of rules in a rulebook you deliberately selected for having long chains. It also kills the two authored seams the audit found: build cost stops being a hand-written table (a building's cost is computed from how far from ambient it has to hold a value, for how long, over how much material), and 'which chunks become buildings' stops being a taste-driven rule (a chunk becomes a building exactly when it can hold a state that some rule needs).

**Example surprise** — Night 612 your phone says: 'MARSHHOUSE — first of its kind, at Low Ford. Holds slot 11 above 41 for 900 ticks. Two sleeping rules woke. New stuff: the grey (slot 3 near zero, slot 9 at 88).' You have no idea what slot 9 is. Four months later a different tribe, in a different part of the valley, gets slot 9 above 80 by an unrelated route, and a third rule fires that quietly moves slot 2 between neighbouring tiles. The valley has just discovered that something conducts through the ground. Neither you nor anyone else knew that was in there.

**Ceiling or rate** — Ceiling, directly, and it is the biggest ceiling move available. Today the maximum possible depth of the technology stack is a number you can count on your fingers by reading your own rules. After this, the maximum is the chain depth of a generated rulebook that you SCREENED for chain depth — and if a valley turns out shallow, you draw another one. It raises the rate too, because more live rules means more things worth doing, but the ceiling is the point.

**How it works** — A material is a row of about 16 unnamed numbers, slot 0 to slot 15. You never call slot 4 'temperature'. A rule is a row: {trigger: slot p, comparator, threshold T, held for D ticks}, {context: which material classes must be present, optional second slot condition}, {effect: slot r changes by set/add/multiply/transfer with coefficient c}, {optional byproduct: a new material class}, {optional consumption: slot q drains at rate k}. You author six or so rule SHAPES; the seed draws p, q, r, T, D, c, and the class masks. Draw 200-400 rules per valley.

Most rules are asleep — no raw material and no bare-hand command produces a value of slot p above T for D ticks. Waking one requires a building that can hold a state. So a works declares an envelope: 'I hold slot 4 at 900 or above, for up to 300 ticks, over material class C.' Every rule whose trigger fits inside that envelope becomes locally active on that tile. Build cost falls straight out of the envelope: cost = (distance from the tile's ambient value) × (duration) × (mass of material that must be held), paid in the same interpreter-steps and material-mass the sim already counts. No recipe table anywhere.

The hard part is that random rules mostly give you a dead world, so add a WORLD LATHE that runs before a valley is committed. Represent 'what states are reachable' as one interval per slot (a low and a high number). Start from the raw materials the terrain generator places plus the effects of the 40 bare-hand commands. Then loop: which rules can fire given the current intervals? Firing them widens the intervals. Repeat until nothing changes. Score the candidate rulebook by (a) how many rounds it took to stop growing — that is your storey count, and you want 8 or more; (b) how many distinct rules fired in each round; and (c) whether there is a group of rules whose outputs supply each other's inputs, which is literally the 'buildings that make the material for other buildings' threshold. That last test is the RAF algorithm from origin-of-life chemistry (Hordijk and Steel): repeatedly delete any rule whose inputs are unreachable, until nothing more deletes, and see what survives. It is polynomial time and about forty lines. Draw a thousand candidate rulebooks overnight on the CPU, keep the two or three with the deepest chains and a self-feeding group, then never look at their contents again.

Keep four to six hand-written anchor rules (fire, water, breaking) so the valley has a familiar spine and the generated rules hang off something you recognise.

The ritual: THE UNSEALING, once a month, thirty seconds. The world shows you three sleeping rules that are closest to reachable — it shows you only what a building would have to HOLD to trigger them, never what they do. You pick one and it gets a discount for a season. You are blessing your world with a law neither of you has read.

**Authored risk** — The rule SHAPES are yours, and that is a real seam — six shapes is a taste decision and it decides what kinds of physics are even expressible. If none of your shapes can express 'this substance carries information', writing is never getting invented, generated rulebook or not. Also: the anchor rules are hand-written and the tribe's first three weeks will be spent on them, so the early game is still your game. The harsh version: you have moved authorship up one level and multiplied its reach by thirty, but you have not eliminated it, and a critic could fairly say you authored the space and the seed picked a point in it. The genuine difference is that you can no longer predict what happens, which is the property you actually care about.

**Build cost** — The biggest single item here. Rule row format, generator, and the interpreter that checks live rules against tile state: maybe 1,500 lines. The world lathe with interval reachability plus the RAF-style pruning: another 600. Ripping out the hand-written build-cost table and computing cost from envelopes touches everything. ### The Itch  `core` · 8/10

Every audit of every design in the pool said the same thing: you do not run out of compression, you run out of PROBLEMS. Hunger, cold and breakage are three problems, and after ten weeks they are solved. So make the world compute its own problems, from three sources that never dry up. First: the world knows which of its own sleeping rules are nearly reachable, so it asks for exactly the state that would wake one — without saying what waking it does. Second: success eats resources, so every time a building consumes the last of something, the world asks 'do that again without it'. Third: when the river or a fire destroys a building, the world asks for its effect back, on a landscape that has changed.

**What becomes possible** — The tribe gets a stream of goals that sit exactly one step past what it can currently do, forever, generated by the state of the world rather than by you typing them. It converts your success into your next problem: the more they build, the faster they deplete, the sooner they must find a substitute — and there is no saturation point in a goal generator driven by consumption. It also means that at month eighteen there are still things the valley has never done, and the world knows where they are.

**Example surprise** — Night 903: 'SUBSTITUTION — the redbank clay is gone. Millbank has failed this for 41 nights.' Night 907: 'Millbank did it. It holds slot 11 using ash from the smoke-house instead. The new version is three commands shorter and 20% cheaper than the original.' The world posed a problem it created by depleting itself, and the answer came back better than the thing it replaced. Nobody wrote 'invent a cheaper kiln lining'.

**Ceiling or rate** — Mostly RATE, and it is the single biggest rate raiser on this list — without it everything plateaus at week ten regardless of how deep the rulebook is. But it does raise the ceiling in one honest sense: a deep generated rulebook is worthless if the tribe never gets pointed at the doorway. Pointing the search at nearly-reachable rules is the difference between climbing an eight-storey rulebook in two years and climbing it in twenty. Depth you cannot reach in a human timescale is not a ceiling, it is decoration.

**How it works** — Three generators writing into one goal table: {id, kind, target (slot, threshold, duration) or (material to avoid) or (effect to restore), created_night, best_achieved, budget_multiple_needed, last_movement_night, state}.

GENERATOR 1, DOORWAYS. Each night, run the same interval-reachability loop the world lathe uses, but on the actual current world: current works, current materials, current terrain. That gives you the reachable box per slot. Then walk the sleeping rules and compute, for each, the gap between its trigger and the reachable box: how much further slot p has to go, and how much longer it must be held. Emit the smallest few gaps as goals of the form 'get slot p above T and hold it D ticks, anywhere'. Never tell the tribe or yourself what the rule does. Weight goals by how many OTHER sleeping rules would come within reach if this one woke — prefer doorways that open rooms, not doorways into cupboards.

GENERATOR 2, SCARCITY. Every material deposit is a stock with a drain rate. Fit a straight line to the last 50 nights of consumption. When a material that current works depend on is projected to run out within 200 nights, emit 'achieve the same measured effect without material class X'. Effect equality is checked mechanically: run old and new against a fixed set of probe situations and compare the resulting property changes.

GENERATOR 3, WOUNDS. When works are destroyed, emit 'restore this effect'. The landscape has changed, so the search will not find the same answer.

GATING, which is what stops the table filling with junk. A goal is kept only if it is unsolved at the standard nightly search budget AND shows progress at three times the budget. Solved twice cheaply, it is retired. Sample which goals get search time in proportion to how much the best-achieved number MOVED over the last ten nights — the absolute size of the change, up or down, is the signal (this is exactly the Absolute Learning Progress measure from the automatic-curriculum work of Portelas and colleagues, and it is about ten lines: keep a short history per goal, take the size of the difference, sample proportionally). A goal with zero movement for 30 nights goes on the shelf, not in the bin — a shelved goal wakes automatically if the reachable box moves toward it.

**Authored risk** — Medium and worth naming honestly. You do not author any specific goal, but you author the three KINDS of goal, and 'reach a state / replace a material / restore a function' is a worldview. There is no goal here that says 'get more people' or 'defend something', so no social or competitive technology will ever be asked for. The gating thresholds (three times budget, 30-night shelf) are taste dressed as mechanism, and you will move them until the goal stream looks the way you hoped, which is exactly what the audit warned about with the stationarity heuristic. The strongest defence is that Generator 2 is entirely driven by the tribe's own consumption, so its goals are genuinely not from you.

**Build cost** — Small if The Sealed Rulebook exists first, because the reachability loop is shared. About 600 lines: three generator functions, the goal table, the gate, and the sampler. A week, mostly spent watching what the goal stream actually looks like on a running valley and being disappointed twice.

**Tuning burden** — Real, and it is the fiddliest of these six. Four dials: how many doorway goals to emit per night, the budget multiple for the gate, the shelf window, and the doorway weighting. Fiddling here is dangerous because it directly shapes what gets invented — the difference between 'interesting goal stream' and 'you chose the tech tree in slow motion' is about four dial-moves. ### The Night Shift  `core` · 7/10

Three unglamorous pieces that decide whether any of the above is real. First, run the candidate programs on the graphics card instead of one at a time on the processor — a hundred thousand candidates stepping through their instructions in lockstep, which is a thirty-year-old trick from genetic programming and is worth ten to a hundred times the throughput. Second, steer the search with a simple counting table rather than blind guessing, because past three layers deep blind guessing stalls. Third, and most important: stamp every remembered answer with a version number for the site it was computed at, and only reuse it if the version matches exactly — because otherwise the search silently reuses answers from a world that no longer exists and your map goes quietly wrong six weeks later.

**What becomes possible** — Deeper programs per night, which is the same thing as more storeys climbed per year. And a project you can still believe at month eighteen. The version stamps sound like plumbing but they are the difference between a two-year run and a two-month run followed by six weeks of hunting a bug that never crashes anything.

**Example surprise** — The surprise this one makes possible is a negative that becomes a positive: night 300, the paranoid re-check fires — 'MISMATCH at Ovenbank: remembered result disagrees with fresh run' — and you find in twenty minutes a bug that would otherwise have quietly poisoned four months of history. On the positive side, going from three layers to five layers deep per night is what lets a chunk that calls two neighbouring works get found at all, which is the whole 'workshop becomes village' rung.

**Ceiling or rate** — Ceiling, in the most literal sense available: how many layers deep the search reaches per night is the hard cap on how complicated the deepest invention can be, and this raises it by one to two layers. That does not sound like much until you remember each layer multiplies. The version stamps raise a different ceiling — the number of months you can run before you stop trusting the output — and for a two-year target that is the actually binding constraint. I will not pretend this system generates surprises. It raises how big the others can get.

**How it works** — (a) BATCH THE EVALUATOR. The expensive thing is not choosing candidate programs, it is running them against the world. Flatten each candidate into an array of one-byte instruction codes (40 commands fits in six bits) and give each a small private probe state of a few hundred bytes. Step them in lockstep: every candidate executes its slot-0 instruction, then slot-1, and so on. One warp on the 5070 handles 32 candidates. The classic result here is Langdon's SIMD genetic-programming interpreter, which hit roughly a billion program-operations per second on 2008 hardware; you will do far better. Realistic gain over a Python tree-walker is 100× or more; over a competent Rust tree-walker, 10-50×. The killer is divergence — candidates that branch differently waste lanes — so group candidates by SHAPE (same instruction sequence, different arguments) so a whole batch follows one control path. This is natural because the search generates by shape anyway.

VRAM NOTE: the 8GB card cannot hold the batch evaluator and the naming model comfortably at once. Do not try. The naming model runs once, at dawn, for about two minutes, in one batched call; the evaluator owns the card the other twenty-three hours and fifty-eight minutes.

(b) STEER WITH A TABLE. Keep counts: for each (parent command, child command, goal kind) triple, how many times did that pair appear in a program that worked. Score candidate expansions by that count. A few megabytes, updates for free nightly, no training. Be honest with yourself about its reach: counting pairs gets you to about layer four or five. If you want seven, you need a small learned model that looks at the goal and predicts which commands to try, and that is a real project — call it a month, and do not start it until the table has visibly plateaued.

(c) VERSION-STAMPED MEMORY, the important one. Every site carries an integer context_version. It increments whenever ANY works within reach of that site is built, completes, decays a stage, falls down, or when the river changes the tile. Every remembered result stores the (site, context_version) it was computed under. A lookup hits only on an exact integer match. Do not be clever — do not try to work out which changes 'could not possibly have mattered'. Cleverness here is precisely where the silent wrong answers live, and an integer compare is a bug you cannot have. Stale entries are never invalidated eagerly; they simply stop matching, and a background sweep drops anything more than K versions old. You will lose cache hits every time a building goes up. Pay it.

PARANOID MODE: one lookup in a thousand re-runs the program anyway and screams if the answers differ. Costs 0.1% of throughput and converts an entire class of invisible bugs into a loud alarm. Leave it on forever.

(d) COMPRESSION IS NOT EFFICIENCY, and this belongs here. The compressor ranks repeated chunks by how many characters of code they save. That is not what you want — a four-command chunk can loop forty thousand times. So after the compressor proposes its candidates, re-rank them by ACTUAL STEPS SAVED across all the programs that used them (you already measured every program's step count when you ran it), and only accept chunks that pay in steps as well as in length. Two hundred lines, and it stops the compressor and survival pressure from pulling in opposite directions. While you are there: do not let the naming model name anything until the chunk has been called at at least three distinct sites, and name it from what CHANGED IN THE WORLD the first time it ran, never from what its code looks like. That alone kills most of the three-in-four plumbing problem.

**Authored risk** — Almost none — this system does not decide any content. The one place taste creeps in is the acceptance threshold for chunks in (d): set it high and you get a small clean library that misses things, set it low and you get a field of junk buildings. That is a dial that shapes what exists, so it counts.

**Build cost** — The batch evaluator is the single hardest engineering item across all six systems: 1,500-2,500 lines of careful kernel work plus a matching reference implementation on the processor that must produce bit-identical results, because you will need it to check the card is not lying. Three to four weeks, and the highest chance of misery. The counting table is 100 lines. The version stamps are about 50 lines and MUST be built before anything else on this entire list — retrofitting them into a running project is how you spend a month bug-hunting. Item (d) is 200 lines.

**Tuning burden** — Low, which is a relief. Batch size, the sweep depth for old memory entries, and the steps-saved threshold in (d). None of them are taste calls except the last. The real burden here is not tuning, it is that a wrong kernel produces plausible-looking wrong results, so build the reference implementation first and diff against it on every change forever.

**Interactions** — Everything sits on top of this. The Sealed Rulebook is only worth having if the search can climb it. The River Remembers must increment context versions or it silently corrupts the memory. Five Valleys competes for the same GPU and the same nightly budget. The Second People's decompilation uses the same evaluator. Build (c) and (d) first, in week one, before any of the exciting systems; build (a) when the processor version is measurably the bottleneck, which will be within a month.

**Chance it does nothing** — The version stamps and the steps-saved re-ranking: near 0% chance they do nothing — they are load-bearing and everything else is untrustworthy without them. The batch evaluator: maybe 35% chance it delivers far less than hoped, because your candidate programs touch a big shared world state rather than a tiny private input, and if the probe state does not fit in a few hundred bytes per candidate the whole lockstep trick falls apart on memory bandwidth. Test that assumption in an afternoon before committing three weeks. And the honest structural caveat that applies to this whole system: making the search faster brings any plateau FORWARD rather than raising it — it only raises the ceiling if The Itch and The Sealed Rulebook are supplying something worth searching for. Speed on its own just gets you bored sooner.

### The River Remembers  `strong` · 8/10

Put a real, cheap landscape under the valley: a grid of ground heights and loose sediment, water routed downhill every night, dirt picked up where the current is strong and dropped where it is weak. Rivers modelled this way meander, cut off bends, and jump their banks on their own — you do not script a flood. The important twist is that BUILDINGS CHANGE THE GROUND: a weir drops sediment upstream, a quarry cuts a hole, a cleared terrace sends more water downhill faster. So three hundred nights of successful building quietly changes where the river wants to be, and then one night it goes there.

**What becomes possible** — Catastrophe stops being a dice roll and becomes a consequence. The valley can destroy itself with its own success, on a delay long enough that the cause is invisible until you scrub the timelapse back. It also gives archaeology a physical cause — sediment buries ruins and erosion exposes them, so WHICH ruins become readable is decided by geology rather than by a random roll — and it makes the material supply move, because cutting down through the ground exposes a different rock layer than the one that was there before.

**Example surprise** — Night 1,340: 'AVULSION. The Ovenbank channel has jumped 600 metres east. Eleven works are dry. Three are underwater.' You scrub the timelapse and watch the sediment wedge build up behind the four weirs the tribe put in around night 1,010 — the weirs raised the riverbed until the river was running above its own floodplain, and then it left. Two months later: 'the new channel cut a bank at Low Ford and exposed a ruin from the second people; the tribe has read it and rebuilt a works type that has not stood for 400 nights.' Nothing in that sentence was authored.

**Ceiling or rate** — Ceiling on SURPRISE SIZE, not on technology depth. It is the only system here that can invalidate a whole civilisation's foundations in one night in a way that is causally traceable to their own choices — that is the biggest single event this world can produce. It also raises the sustained rate indirectly by killing ossification, which the Glyphworks audit named as what makes months three to twelve boring: cheap infrastructure makes the search lazy and the tribe stops exploring. A river that eats the infrastructure is the antidote, and unlike a random disaster it is fair.

**How it works** — Under the ~400 sites, a 256×256 or 512×512 grid of {ground_height, loose_sediment, rock_class_by_depth, water}. Each site is anchored to a cell. Every night, run about 50 passes of a reduced-complexity landscape model — this is the CAESAR family of cellular models, which are known to produce meanders, cutoffs and braiding from purely local rules:
1. Route the night's rainfall downhill, splitting flow between all downhill neighbours in proportion to slope.
2. At each cell compute stream power roughly as discharge × slope.
3. Move sediment: if stream power exceeds what the cell is currently carrying, erode; if not, deposit. Cap both.
4. Lateral cut: a channel cell erodes its outside-of-bend neighbour faster than its inside one. This is what makes meanders wander.
5. Avulsion check: if a channel cell's bed has built up more than H above the lowest floodplain cell within a short radius, the channel jumps there. A flood is simply a night with high rainfall; it interacts with whatever is standing because levees and works are part of the height field.

Works write into the grid. Give each works an explicit geomorphic footprint derived from its rule envelope — one that holds water raises the local water surface and multiplies deposition upstream; one that consumes rock lowers ground_height and adds loose sediment downstream; one that consumes plant matter raises runoff on its cell. Deposits deplete: a cell's rock_class at a given depth has a finite quantity, and when it is gone you must dig deeper, which costs more, which feeds The Itch's scarcity generator.

Ruins: each ruin carries buried_depth, which is just the sediment deposited above it since it fell. Readability falls off with depth and comes back when erosion cuts down to it.

The cost is trivial — a 256² grid × 50 passes is nothing next to the program search — so run it every night and store the height field once a week for the timelapse.

**Authored risk** — Two honest seams. First, the geomorphic footprint of each works is a small hand-written mapping from 'what the building holds' to 'what it does to the ground', which is exactly the kind of table the audit called a recipe book in disguise — it is much smaller than a build-cost table, but you will be tempted to exaggerate the footprints so the feedback loop actually bites, and if you do, you authored the disaster. Second, the erosion and avulsion constants decide whether the river is a dramatist or wallpaper, and there is no principled way to set them. Everything else here is genuinely mechanical: nobody writes 'flood on night 1340'.

**Build cost** — Cheap and self-contained. About 600-800 lines: the grid, flow routing, erode/deposit, lateral cut, avulsion, plus the works footprint hook and the burial bookkeeping. One to two weeks. Almost no runtime cost. Best fun-per-line on this list.

**Tuning burden** — Moderate, and unavoidable, but pleasant — this is the kind of tuning where you watch a timelapse and go 'too fast' or 'too slow'. Four dials: erosion rate, deposition cap, avulsion height threshold, flood frequency. Budget a month of occasional adjustment. The trap: tuning it while a valley is running changes the physics under a live tribe, so keep a scratch valley for tuning and only push settings to the real one at an age boundary.

**Interactions** — Feeds The Itch (destroyed works become goals; exposed rock layers change what materials exist; depletion drives substitution goals). Feeds The Second People (burial and exposure decide which ruins are readable, and floods are a common cause of collapse). Fights The Night Shift's memory: every time the river changes a tile, that tile's context version must increment, or the search will happily reuse an answer from a world that had a river in it. Does not need The Sealed Rulebook, so this one is buildable first and standalone if you want an early win.

**Chance it does nothing** — Low that it adds nothing at all — maybe 10% — because a moving river on a six-month timelapse is worth watching regardless. But about 35% that the specific thing you want, works causing their own flood, never fires: the footprints are too weak, or the tribe never builds enough in one basin, or the avulsion threshold is set so the channel jumps for its own reasons long before the weirs matter. If that happens you will be tempted to crank the footprints until it does happen, and at that moment the surprise becomes yours rather than the world's. Decide in advance that you will not do that.

### The Second People  `strong` · 8/10

When a tribe dies — and they will, from upkeep exceeding production, or a flood, or the ore running out — everything they knew dies with them. What stays is the landscape they changed and the buildings they left standing. A new tribe seeds in with nothing but the forty bare-hand commands, on a valley covered in machinery it does not understand. Here is the good part: it can USE a building long before it can read one, because using it just means calling it. So a second people operates inherited machinery it cannot build, and slowly works backwards to the recipe by watching what the machine does.

**What becomes possible** — Technology outliving its inventors, which is the only way a stack gets deeper than one tribe's search can climb in its own lifetime. It also makes the whole invention problem fresh again without resetting the world — a new people re-derives things in a different order, by different routes, against a landscape that has since moved. And it produces the reconstruction diff: the printed difference between what the original chunk was and what the new people worked out it must have been.

**Example surprise** — 'AGE 4, NIGHT 61. The people at Low Ford have been running the Greyhouse for forty nights without knowing what it is. Tonight they read it. Their version differs in one command — and the wrong version makes something the original never made.' A misreading of an inherited machine producing a new material is the exact shape of real history, and it is not authorable: it requires a partial recovery, a search that fills the holes, and a rulebook you had not fully explored.

**Ceiling or rate** — Both, and honestly the ceiling more than I expected. Rate, because every reseed makes invention fresh on a world that is not fresh, which is the main answer to 'what happens in month nine when everything obvious is invented'. Ceiling, because the built environment does the teaching: the second people start on a rung the first people took four hundred nights to reach, so across three or four ages the stack can climb past what any single tribe's search could ever get to. That is the mechanism by which a two-year run beats a two-month run by more than a factor of twelve.

**How it works** — COLLAPSE IS NOT SCRIPTED. It is simply what happens when a tribe's upkeep in materials and agent-time exceeds what it produces and population reaches zero. Over-building is a real way to die and you should not soften it.

WHAT PERSISTS: works rows (with decay continuing), the terrain grid, depleted deposit levels, and ruin rows. What does not persist: the library, the search memory, the goal table.

RUIN ROWS: {works_id, site, body_with_holes, damage_mask, buried_depth, age_number}. The damage mask is generated by PHYSICAL CAUSE, not a random roll. Fire burns out the parts of the body that touch materials with low fire resistance. Flood erases whatever sat below the recorded water line. Time punches holes at a rate set by the durability of the material the works was built from. So what survives is decided by the physics, which means a stone works and a timber works leave different kinds of evidence and you did not decide which.

USING BEFORE READING: calling a works is just calling it — the works holds a state and the local rules fire. The new tribe's search will find that calling the Greyhouse helps long before it knows what the Greyhouse contains. Do nothing to prevent this; it is the best thing in the system.

DECOMPILATION IS A SEARCH, NOT A LOOKUP. Given a holey body and the MEASURED effect of the standing works (which the tribe gets by using it — the property changes it causes), search for a completion whose measured effect matches. This is the same synthesis engine the tribe already runs, pointed at a different question, with the holes as the shape and the observed effects as the target. Store the diff between the recovered body and the original — you have both, the tribe does not — and print it.

AGE STAMPS: every row carries an age number. The map view gets an age slider; ruins from two ages back render darker. That slider is most of the hour-long binge.

A new tribe seeds either from a neighbouring valley's overflow, from a surviving family, or from your god-nudge. Never reset the terrain, the deposits, or the ruins.

**Authored risk** — Low, and this is one of the cleanest. The damage-from-physical-cause rule is the only real seam and it is small. The bigger honesty problem is not authorship but repetition: if the landscape channels the second people hard enough, they will re-derive the same things in the same order, and you will have built a machine for watching the same film four times with different subtitles. That is not authored content, but it feels like it, which is worse.

**Build cost** — Moderate. Ruin rows and physical damage masks: 250 lines. Decompilation reuses the main search engine, so it is maybe 200 lines of glue plus the effect-matching target. Age stamps everywhere: tedious but shallow. The map's age slider is real front-end work, a few days. One to two weeks total. Note this system is nearly free if you are already building The Sealed Rulebook, because 'match the observed effect' is exactly the effect-fingerprint machinery Five Valleys needs.

**Tuning burden** — Light, with one exception. Dials: decay rates, damage-mask severity, how long before a new tribe seeds. The exception is the seeding gap — too short and there is no sense of loss, too long and the works have all fallen down and there is nothing to inherit, and the goldilocks band is narrow and depends on your decay rate. Expect to get this wrong twice.

**Interactions** — Needs The River Remembers to decide burial and exposure, and to supply collapse causes that are not just economic. Needs The Sealed Rulebook to be worth doing — with twelve hand-written rules, the second people just re-find your four storeys. Feeds Five Valleys, since a dead valley is a museum for later wanderers. Its decompilation search competes for the same nightly budget as ordinary invention, so give it a fixed small slice rather than letting it bid.

The mitigations are real but partial — the river has moved, the deposits are depleted, the goal table is regenerated from a different world state, and the rulebook has unexplored parts. ### Five Valleys, One Wanderer  `later` · 4/10

Run four to six valleys, each with its own generated rulebook drawn from the same shapes — a shared pool of about sixty rules everyone has, plus a hundred and fifty private ones each. They do not run at the same speed: one valley is hot and gets most of the machine for a week, the rest tick over slowly, then you rotate. Very occasionally a person walks between valleys carrying a handful of things they know. When something arrives, the sim automatically re-runs it and measures how much of its effect survives — which tells you, as a number, whether that invention was a real law or a local trick.

**What becomes possible** — Five independent draws at a deep rulebook instead of one, which matters because the best thing that ever happens in this project is the maximum across valleys, not the average. It also gives you a novelty test that cannot saturate: a thing invented in valley three that has never appeared in one, two, four or five is genuinely new in a way that a first-in-this-valley is not. And it makes possible one specific event that nothing else here can produce — a technology that means something different when it moves.

**Example surprise** — 'WANDERER. Someone walked from Ashen Fork to Windward with four things. Three are tricks — they do nothing here. One is a LAW: it is the first works Windward has ever had that holds slot 6 above 60, and Windward has been stuck at 58 for 300 nights.' Or the better one, which only this system can produce: 'MUTANT. The Ashen Fork drying-frame works here, but it does not dry anything — in Windward's rulebook the same held state triggers a different rule and it makes the floor hard. Windward is now building drying-frames as paving.' A technology whose meaning changed in transit. You could not have written that.

**Ceiling or rate** — Ceiling, by raising the number of independent tries. The deepest stack you ever see across two years is the maximum over valleys, and a single valley has a nasty habit of settling into one basin and staying there — the audit called it ossification and named it as the month-three-to-twelve killer. Five valleys means five chances to escape it, and only one has to work. The transplant test is a rate contribution rather than a ceiling one, but it is close to free.

**How it works** — Each valley is a fully separate state: terrain grid, rulebook, material deposits, tribes, library, works, goal table, and search memory. Nothing is shared except the code and your reading list.

SCHEDULING. You do not have five times the compute, so do not pretend. One HOT valley gets about 70% of the nightly search budget for a week. Two WARM valleys get 12% each. The rest are COLD at about 3% — they advance a night or two per real day, purely so they exist, are different, and can receive migrants. Rotate the hot slot weekly. This is a genuine sacrifice: the hot-valley timeline moves at roughly two-thirds normal speed, so if the interesting thing was at night 800 you now reach it about 50% later in wall-clock. I think that trade is right given you explicitly value a rare big surprise over a steady drip, but it is a real cost and you should feel it before agreeing.

RULEBOOK OVERLAP. Draw a common pool of ~60 rules that every valley gets, then ~150 private per valley. Without overlap, nothing ever transplants and the wanderer is pointless; with total overlap the valleys are the same place twice.

THE WANDERER. A few times a year, or as your god-nudge, one agent crosses carrying at most K library entries (say 5) and at most one works blueprint. K is the bottleneck and it is the interesting parameter.

THE TRANSPLANT TEST, which is the cheap and lovely bit. Keep, for every named chunk, an EFFECT FINGERPRINT: run it against a fixed set of about 30 probe situations at home and record the vector of property changes it caused. On arrival, run the same probes in the new valley and compare. Score is the fraction of the original effects preserved. Above 0.8 → LAW. Below 0.2 → TRICK. In between, or effects preserved on DIFFERENT slots → MUTANT, which is the one worth a push notification. This is one table, one function, and it answers 'was this real or local' with a number, nightly, forever.

CARGO CULT falls out for free: if the migrant's library says a blueprint is worth building, the search may build it here even though the rule it depends on does not exist in this rulebook. The building goes up, costs upkeep, does nothing, and is eventually abandoned. Do not special-case this. Let it happen and let the map show it.

**Authored risk** — Low on content and this is one of the cleanest systems here — nobody authors what transplants or what survives. Harsher point: choosing which bundle walks is you steering the world once a month, and if you always pick the bundle you find interesting you are hand-cranking cultural diffusion. The scheduler and hard state isolation are the work — 600-800 lines and a genuine risk of one valley's state leaking into another, which is exactly the class of silent bug you dread. The effect fingerprint and transplant test are maybe 150 lines. One to two weeks. Do not build this until a single valley has run for a month, because five copies of a broken thing is five times the debugging.

**Tuning burden** — Light after setup, which is unusual on this list. Dials: the hot/warm/cold split, rotation period, shared-pool size, K (how much a wanderer carries), and crossing frequency. Set them once, revisit at month three. The one you will genuinely agonise over is the compute split, because it is the only place in this project where you must choose between depth and breadth with real money.

**Interactions** — Needs The Sealed Rulebook — with hand-written physics all valleys have the same laws and the whole law-versus-trick measurement is meaningless, which is why this system is worthless on its own. ### THE BLANK RULEBOOK — physics as rows a building can add  `core` · 8/10

The audit's fix for "you only get three to five levels of technology and then sprawl" is to stop writing finished physics rules and instead write about six rule shapes with blanks in them, then let a building fill in the blanks with numbers it can physically hold. My job is making that fast and making it safe. Rules stop being code and become rows in a table: a building going up adds rows, a building falling down removes them. Because rules are now data the world reads rather than code the world runs, the ledger fingerprints them automatically — a remembered answer from before the kiln existed is thrown away without one line of special-case logic.

**What becomes possible** — The number of levels of technology stops being something he counted when he wrote the physics file. A building can push a blank to a value nothing has ever held, and that value is a genuinely new physical regime that appears as a number nowhere in his source code. Second-order buildings — a works whose upkeep can only be paid with something another works makes — become detectable mechanically rather than hoped for.

**Example surprise** — Night 214: "A works at site 61 declares it holds blank 2 of shape SUSTAIN at 340 for 40 grains. The highest anyone has ever held is 96 for 9 grains. Something came out of it with hardness 71. The previous all-time hardness in this valley was 44, and the number 71 appears nowhere in your rules file." ### THE LEDGER — remembering answers without lying about which world they came from  `core` · 7/10

The search must not redo work, so it remembers "I ran this program at this spot and here is what happened." The danger the audit named is that what a program does depends on which buildings are standing, and buildings get built and burn down, so a remembered answer can quietly belong to a world that no longer exists. The fix: while a program runs, every fact it looks at gets folded into a running 64-bit number, so at the end you hold a fingerprint meaning "this answer is true in any world where these exact facts still hold these exact values." To reuse an answer you re-read that short list of facts and check the fingerprint still matches; if a weir decayed overnight it does not match, and the answer is thrown away. Nobody ever has to decide in advance which buildings mattered, which is the part the original design got wrong.

**What becomes possible** — The valley can safely have buildings that come and go, and a rulebook that changes underfoot (system 4), without the search silently answering from a dead world. It also creates a specific event that is otherwise impossible: a correct rediscovery. When the river moves back after 200 nights, the old answers are correctly marked stale rather than resumed, so the tribe genuinely re-derives — and the re-derivation can come out different from the original.

**Example surprise** — Night 318: "BANK-THE-COALS is back. Unused since night 41. The flood killed the riverside route 200 nights ago and the remembered answers were correctly refused; tonight the search re-derived it from scratch and the new version is four commands shorter and uses a chunk that did not exist in night 41. Diff attached." A civilisation rediscovering something and getting a better answer the second time is one of the best cards the project can produce, and it only exists if stale answers are detected rather than reused.

**Ceiling or rate** — Directly, and this is the argument I most want him to accept. The ceiling on a surprise is the depth of composition you can trust, because a nine-layer discovery reads more facts than a two-layer one and is therefore the most likely thing to be silently wrong. Without fingerprints the honest maximum trustworthy depth is about two layers, and everything deeper is a coin flip you will never see land. With them there is no depth at which the memory lies. It also deletes the per-site partitioned memory the audit warned would eat 64GB — no context bitsets, no partitioning, no counters to bump by hand.

**How it works** — Give every (address, value) pair a pseudorandom 64-bit tag, computed on the fly rather than stored: tag = splitmix64(seed ^ addr*2654435761 ^ val). This is the trick chess engines have used since 1970 to hash board positions as they change. The VM's read(addr) does fp ^= tag(addr,val) and marks addr in a small bitmap so repeated reads only count once — one XOR and one bit-set, a couple of nanoseconds, invisible next to the rest of the command. A ledger entry is {program_hash: u64, fingerprint: u64, read_addrs: Vec<u32> (usually 10-40), outcome: u32, grains: u32} — roughly 64-120 bytes, so 10 million entries is about 1GB and his 64GB machine holds a year of them; evict least-recently-used past a cap. A lookup hashes the program into one of 256 shards, then walks the entry's read_addrs recomputing the XOR from current values and compares. That is 10-40 memory reads against re-running a 2,000-grain program, so it still pays about 100 to 1 even though checking is not free. Store failures too — about 90% of entries will be "nothing happened" and that is where most of the savings live. Two ways it can still lie, both handled: (a) two different worlds landing on the same 64-bit number — at 10M entries the chance of any collision at all is around one in 700,000, and if that is too loose, go to 128 bits for 8 more bytes per entry; (b) a bug in the fingerprinting itself, which is the real risk — hence the AUDIT SAMPLER: one in every 500 reused answers gets re-executed anyway and compared, and any mismatch dumps the full case to disk and pushes a notification. Those are the forty most valuable lines in the project, because the bug they catch is otherwise discovered eight weeks later as "the map went wrong somehow."

**Authored risk** — Essentially none. The system can only ever say "that answer is stale." The one bias worth naming: the eviction policy quietly favours whichever part of the valley the tribes are currently in, so a too-small memory makes the world slightly more conservative than it should be. Keep it big; RAM is the one resource he has in surplus.

**Build cost** — ~800 lines. One weekend for the fingerprinting and the sharded map, one evening for the audit sampler. The debugging is the cost, not the writing — budget a week chasing the first two mismatches the sampler finds, because they will be real and they will be subtle.

**Tuning burden** — Almost none. The sample rate (1 in 500) and the memory cap. Set once, forget.

**Interactions** — Must exist before system 4. Building a rulebook that changes on top of a memory that cannot notice it changed is the fastest possible route to a map that is quietly wrong for a month. System 1 must funnel every read through one function or the fingerprint has holes. System 5's replay depends on this being correct, and system 6 puts the sampler's mismatch count on the phone.

**Chance it does nothing** — About 15% that it never visibly does anything — because he might have got away without it, and insurance that never pays out looks like waste. That is the wrong test. The failure it prevents is not a crash, it is a map that is subtly wrong for six weeks before anyone notices, and by then every downstream discovery is suspect and the only honest fix is to throw the world away and start again. The chance of that failure happening at least once over six months in a world where buildings decay is, I would say, better than even without this.

### GRAIN — the flat machine  `core` · 6/10

Every tribe program becomes a flat list of 4-byte numbers — one per command, no tree, no pointers — and a small machine written in Rust runs that list. When a program uses a named chunk from the tribe's library, the chunk's commands are pasted straight into the list at assembly time, so calling a chunk costs nothing extra and the machine only ever knows about the ~40 base commands, whether the library holds 40 named chunks or 4,000. Running a program does not copy the valley; it writes into a small scratch pad and keeps a note saying "I changed byte 8,912 from 5 to 9", then walks those notes backwards to undo everything. Everything is charged in grains — a fixed whole number per command — and never in seconds, so a hot laptop and a cold one produce identical history.

**What becomes possible** — The library can grow without limit without the machine getting slower. In the naive build, every named chunk you add is another thing the search must consider and another layer of function-call overhead, so the world quietly grinds to a halt somewhere around 50-150 named chunks — roughly week four. With inlining and a fixed command set, night 200 with a 1,000-entry library runs exactly as fast per program as night 1 did. It also makes a night cheap enough (3-5 minutes) that you can afford to run each night more than once, which is what systems 3 and 5 spend their money on.

**Example surprise** — Night 173, phone card: three tribes in different parts of the valley each solve "keep a coal bed alive until dawn" on the same night, and all three programs are different — 9, 14 and 22 commands, sharing only two chunks. You see all three because the machine had budget left over after the first success instead of stopping there. A fortnight later the 22-command one turns out to be the only one that survives a wet season, and it is the one a cheaper machine would never have found.

**Ceiling or rate** — Mostly rate, and I will not pretend otherwise — a faster machine finds the same shallow things sooner and brings a plateau forward. The one genuine ceiling part is specific: without inlining, library growth throttles the machine, so the world stalls at a library size that cannot express a nine-chunk-deep discovery at all. Making library size free is a precondition for depth, not a source of it. Treat this as the floor everything else stands on rather than as a win in itself.

**How it works** — Instruction = 4 bytes packed in a u32: opcode, destination register, arg a, arg b. A program is a Vec<u32>, capped at 256 instructions, 16 registers. A library entry ("glyph") is a stored instruction run plus its measured grain cost; assembling a program that uses it splices the instructions in with registers renumbered. So the opcode table is frozen at ~40 forever. World state is one large byte array with tiles, agents and stockpiles at fixed known offsets. Every write goes through write(addr,val), which pushes (addr, old_value) onto a per-thread journal Vec and then writes; rewind pops and restores. A typical attempt touches under 200 addresses, so resetting between attempts costs microseconds no matter how big the valley is. Dispatch is a plain Rust match — published dispatch benchmarks show that on modern CPUs a match is within noise of the old computed-goto trick, so no unsafe tricks are needed. Threading: one OS thread per physical core, each with its own arena and journal; the world is read-only truth during a night, so there are no locks anywhere except the ledger (system 2), which is split into 256 independent shards. Expected numbers on his machine: 30-80 million command executions per second per core, ~2,000 grains for an average attempt including physics, 8 physical cores, giving roughly 150,000-300,000 program attempts per second machine-wide. 10 million attempts is about a minute; a 40-million-attempt night is 3-5 minutes. Nothing in the simulation is ever allowed to read the clock; the only clock in the project belongs to the watchdog.

**Authored risk** — One real seam and it is sharp. If he hand-writes 40 numbers for what each command costs in grains, he has quietly decided which technologies are affordable — a recipe book with the recipes filed off, the same crime as the build-cost table the audit flagged. The fix is not optional: derive grain cost mechanically as (facts read + 2 × facts written + 1), measured at run time, so a command's cost falls out of what it actually touches rather than out of his intuition about kilns. That leaves the nightly grain budget as the only authored number. And be blunt about the part engineering cannot fix: choosing which 40 commands exist is authorship, permanently, and no VM design launders it.

**Build cost** — ~4,000 lines of Rust. Two or three weekends to something that runs, then a solid week of profiling — the undo journal and the register renumbering during inlining are where bugs hide. Add a week to write a slow, obviously-correct reference version of the same machine in plain Python plus a test that runs 100,000 random programs through both and demands identical results. That reference machine pays for itself ten times over.

**Tuning burden** — Low once the cost rule is mechanical. He sets the nightly grain budget and the arena size and leaves them alone. The temptation to resist is hand-adjusting individual command costs when a technology he wants is not appearing — that is the most likely route by which this project quietly becomes authored.

**Interactions** — Everything sits on it. The ledger (2) needs every read to run through one chokepoint function. The guide (3) needs feature extraction from the flat instruction array, which is trivial precisely because it is flat. The blank rulebook (4) needs rule lookups to go through the same fingerprinted read path. The counterfactual runs (5) are only affordable because a night is minutes.

**Chance it does nothing** — Near zero that it fails technically; maybe 30% that it makes things feel worse for the first month, because a 100× faster machine finds the same forty shallow things 100× sooner and you hit the boring plateau in week three instead of month three. That is the honest shape of it: this system does not create depth, it removes the machine as an excuse, and what is left is the design problem.

### DOUBT — replaying nights that never happened  `strong` · 7/10

Because a night is a fixed number of grains, a fixed seed and a recorded score table, any night can be re-run and must produce a byte-identical world. Spare capacity — and there will be plenty, since a night costs three to five minutes — gets spent re-running last night with exactly one thing changed: the kiln was not there, the flood did not happen, the seed was different. Comparing outcomes tells you whether a discovery was forced or lucky, and whether one specific building caused it. That is a fact about his world that no amount of running it forwards can ever give him.

**What becomes possible** — A whole class of event that otherwise cannot exist: a claim about cause inside his own world that he did not author and could not have guessed. NECESSARY (appeared in 7 of 8 re-runs), LUCKY (1 of 8), and CAUSED-BY (vanishes in all 8 when you delete one building from the starting world). That last one is how the craft-becomes-industry moment gets proven instead of asserted from a nice-looking map.

**Example surprise** — Night 260, morning card: "THE SMELTER WAS LUCK. Re-ran last night eight times with different seeds — it appeared once. Then re-ran it eight more times with the weir at site 214 removed from the starting world — it appeared zero times. The weir is load-bearing for the smelter, and nothing in the map showed that." Learning that a building three generations old and apparently unrelated is the hinge of your metallurgy is exactly the kind of unauthored, unpredictable fact the whole project is for.

**Ceiling or rate** — Sideways, and I want to be exact rather than generous. It does not make the world do bigger things. It makes a kind of event exist that forward simulation cannot produce at all, and it removes the doubt that otherwise quietly kills the experience — six months in, when a building appears, he will want to know whether it was real or noise, and without this he will never know. Since the objective is felt surprise, a mechanism that certifies a surprise as real is doing work directly on the objective.

**How it works** — Each night writes one log row: {night, dawn_world_hash, seed, grain_budget, guide_score_blob_hash, accepted_ids[], dusk_world_hash}. Dawn snapshots (the SQLite file plus the world byte array, a few hundred MB) are kept 14 daily, then 8 weekly, then monthly — about 30GB over six months. SELF-CHECK: every night, pick a random past night, re-run it from its dawn snapshot, compare the dusk hash. Costs one night's compute — three minutes. A mismatch means a determinism bug, and finding it within days instead of months is the entire reason to build this before you need it. Their world changes are thrown away — they never touch real history — and all that is kept is a small summary row saying which of tonight's discoveries appeared in how many variants. This is cheap precisely because of GRAIN's arena design: a variant run is just another pass over the same read-only dawn world with a different journal. **Authored risk** — Near zero. It counts things and reports counts. The only judgement in it is which world fact to delete for the causal test, and "the most recently built works" is a mechanical answer.

**Build cost** — ~600 lines, given systems 1 and 2 exist. A weekend. The snapshot retention policy and an actual restore drill are half of it — a backup nobody has restored is not a backup.

**Tuning burden** — Trivial. How many variants (8 is fine), what share of nightly capacity goes to them (10-20%), and the extremeness threshold for pushing to the phone.

**Interactions** — Depends on GRAIN (1) for cheap re-runs; on THE LEDGER (2) for the memory not to leak across variants — run each variant against its own view or the first variant poisons the rest, which is the one real bug hiding in this system; and on THE HUNCH (3) logging rounded scores so a replay never needs the graphics card. ### THE PULSE — the process that keeps the world alive and tells you when it is lying  `cheap-win` · 4/10

The world has to survive six months running unattended on a laptop, and the real chore is not tuning, it is a crash at 3am that silently stops history and is discovered a week later. So it runs as two programs: one does nights, and one tiny one watches. The watcher reads a heartbeat file, and if the night number has not moved in three times the usual night length it kills the world, restores the last dawn snapshot, restarts, and tells his phone what happened. The graphics-card parts also run as their own separate programs, so if a driver falls over the world carries on searching blind and simply says so on the card.

**What becomes possible** — Six months of continuous history instead of five weeks of history and seven weeks of a dead process. And, just as important, a phone card that reports whether the world is telling the truth: the ledger's audit-sampler mismatch count and last night's replay self-check both live here. Those two lines are the difference between six months of history he believes and six months he half-suspects.

**Example surprise** — The card reads GUIDE DOWN for three nights after a driver reset. Inventions per night fall from 4 to 0.5 and the map stops changing; when the guide comes back the tribe finds two things in one night. He now knows exactly what the guide is worth, measured by a three-night natural experiment he did not run on purpose. Separately, and this is the one that saves the project: "LEDGER MISMATCH ×3 — night 431" arrives on a Tuesday, and the bug that would otherwise have made the map quietly wrong from night 400 onwards is caught with 31 nights of damage instead of 200.

**Ceiling or rate** — Neither, and I will not dress it up. It raises neither the ceiling nor the rate. It protects the word SUSTAINED in the goal — a world that dies on night 60 and is noticed on night 67 has a rate of zero for a week and, worse, breaks the habit of checking. The honest justification is that the objective is a sustained rate over six months, and this is the only system on the list that defends the denominator.

**How it works** — The world process writes heartbeat.json after each night — {night, dusk_hash, grains, wall_seconds, status} — to a temp file then renamed, so it is never read half-written. The pulse process (~400 lines, no dependencies) polls every 30 seconds, keeps the median of the last 20 wall_seconds values, and if the file has not updated in 3× that median it kills the world, restores the last dawn snapshot, restarts, and pushes. systemd with Restart=always sits underneath as a second layer. The guide net and the naming model each run as their own process behind a local socket with a 5-second timeout; three consecutive timeouts flips the status word to GUIDE_DOWN or NAMER_DOWN and the world continues without them — blind search, and chunks named fn_1204 until the namer returns. The world NEVER blocks on the graphics card; that single rule prevents most 3am hangs. The pulse owns the phone card and should say exactly four things: nights per real day (his "is the laptop keeping up" number — and when it falls, nothing about history changes, there is simply less of it, which is the whole point of budgeting in grains); the ledger audit-sampler mismatch count, which should always be 0 and where any other number is a five-alarm fire; last night's replay self-check result; and whether the card processes are alive. Snapshots: 14 daily, 8 weekly, then monthly, roughly 30GB over six months, with a restore drill performed on day one.

**Authored risk** — Zero. It cannot create or shape content. Its only opinion is when to give up on a hung process.

**Build cost** — ~400 lines and an evening, plus half a day for the snapshot and restore machinery. The cheapest system on this list by a wide margin, and the one most likely to be skipped, which is exactly why it is on the list.

**Tuning burden** — One number: the hang multiplier. Set it to 3× the median night and never think about it again.

**Interactions** — Depends on nothing; everything depends on it staying up. It reports the ledger's sampler count (2) and DOUBT's self-check result (5), and it is what allows the guide and the namer (3) to be treated as optional luxuries rather than load-bearing parts.

**Chance it does nothing** — About 5% that it never fires at all over six months — a laptop running a Rust process and a graphics driver 24/7 for half a year will hang at least once, probably several times. But its upside is strictly bounded: the very best it can do is stop something bad. Do not expect it to be interesting; expect it to be the reason the interesting things are still arriving in month five.

### THE HUNCH — a small net on the graphics card that only ranks next commands  `later` · 5/10

Blind trying stalls at about three commands deep because the number of possible programs explodes. So put a small neural network on the graphics card whose entire job is: given the goal, the last few commands written, and what buildings are within reach, guess which command is worth trying next. It never writes programs and it is never believed — the machine still runs every candidate and keeps only the ones that actually work — so the net can be badly wrong without putting anything false into the world. It is retrained from scratch every night on that day's own hits and misses, which takes about half a minute.

**What becomes possible** — Programs of 30-60 commands, six or seven chunks deep, become findable inside one night's budget. That is the difference between "the tribe found a way to keep a fire in" and "the tribe found a nine-stage process", and the second one is what a second-order building requires.

**Example surprise** — Night 96, push: "KILN-FEED (11 chunks deep, 54 commands). This goal had failed 31 nights running at 60 million tries a night. Tonight it took 380,000 tries. The guide's top-ranked move was heat-before-bind, an order no accepted program had ever used." Nine nights later that program is expensive and place-bound enough to be built as a works, and the map grows a building that exists only because a ranker had a hunch about command order.

**Ceiling or rate** — Yes — the clearest ceiling raise available from this lens, and for a reason worth stating precisely. It does not make anything faster; it changes which depths are reachable at all. A tenfold speedup buys roughly one extra layer of depth, because the space grows exponentially. Guidance that prunes well buys three or four. That is the difference between a valley of tools and a valley where one building feeds another.

**How it works** — The critical decision is FRONTIER WAVES, not per-node calls. Asking the graphics card once per search step is the classic disaster: every call costs roughly 10 microseconds of pure overhead regardless of size, so 10 million calls is 100 seconds of nothing but overhead plus eight stalled CPU cores. Instead the search runs in waves — the CPU collects 65,536 partly-written programs, packs their features into one big matrix, sends it in ONE call, receives 65,536 × 2,048 scores back, then spends the next few hundred milliseconds expanding by those scores. That is 1,000-3,000 card calls a night and maybe two minutes of card time total. Features: 192 numbers — the last 3 command ids looked up in a learned table, the goal type and its numbers, grains remaining, what is in the agent's hands, and which chunks and works are in reach folded down to 32 numbers. Model: lookup table, two hidden layers of 512, output of 2,048 slots, with library entries beyond 2,048 sharing slots by hashing. About 3 million numbers, under 100MB on the card in half precision. Training: each night take today's expansions labelled by whether that branch ever led to something the world accepted — 5-20 million rows — and train from scratch for 2-3 passes in 30-60 seconds. From scratch every night, deliberately: it removes every headache about a growing output layer and stops the net drifting somewhere strange over six months. DETERMINISM, and this detail decides whether the project is replayable at all: floating-point results on a graphics card are not guaranteed identical run to run. So round every score to a 16-bit whole number, and write that entire rounded score table into the night's log. Replaying a night reads the recorded table and never touches the card. "Nearly replayable" is worth nothing, so do this on day one. VRAM discipline: guide ≤ 1GB, naming model ≤ 5GB, 2GB slack, and they live in separate processes so one dying does not take the other with it. ANTI-RUT: 30% of the night's grain budget is spent ignoring the net entirely and picking uniformly at random among legal commands, and that fraction rises automatically when the count of distinct new outcomes this week falls below its trailing average.

**Authored risk** — The sneakiest authorship in the whole design, and worse than a hand-written table because it is invisible. The net is trained on the world's own past, so it makes the future resemble the past — it narrows the reachable space to the familiar, which has exactly the same effect on surprise as a designer's taste, arriving through a feedback loop instead of a text file. And the map still looks healthy while it happens. The forced-random fraction is the only antidote, and its size is a number he sets by feel, so his taste comes back into the project — concentrated into one dial instead of spread across a table. Say it plainly: a guided world is a world with an opinion.

**Build cost** — ~1,200 lines: a Python trainer, a small inference process, and the wave-batching plumbing on the Rust side. One weekend to a working ranker. Then a month of measurement, which is the real cost — you cannot tell by looking whether guidance is helping.

**Tuning burden** — The heaviest of the six, honestly. Explore fraction, how many nights of history to train on, how to label a branch as "led somewhere", when to distrust the net. Expect weekly fiddling for two months. Build the measurement first: run half the night's budget guided and half blind, log which half found what, and put "found by guide / found blind" on the phone card so the dial has a readout.

**Interactions** — Needs GRAIN (1) for flat feature extraction, and needs its scores logged so DOUBT (5) can replay nights exactly. Feeds off THE BLANK RULEBOOK (4), which gives it a much bigger and more interesting space to prune. Shares the 8GB card with the naming model, hence the hard split and separate processes. Mildly reduces the ledger's hit rate, because a guided search goes where nobody has been — which is the point.

**Chance it does nothing** — 40-50% that it is neutral or actively harmful for the first two months. Neural guidance for program search is famously finicky and the training signal here is thin and lopsided — almost every example is a failure. Plan the fallback now: if guided is not beating blind after a month of split-budget measurement, turn the net off and put a plain command-pair frequency table there instead ("after HEAT, what usually comes next"). Fifty lines, no card, and it captures a real share of the benefit. Building the fifty-line version FIRST is probably the correct order.

Then log an 8-byte hash per wave as a tripwire: 24 KB/night instead of 500 GB. Better still, shrink the net until int16 fixed-point CPU inference is affordable, and determinism becomes free while the 8GB card is reserved for the namer. Frontier-wave batching is otherwise the correct call (per-node GPU calls at 5-15 us launch overhead would be a disaster, as they say).
